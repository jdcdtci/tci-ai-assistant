# 0. How to Read This Document

This is a design specification for a software product, not a course build. Version 2.0 extends version 1.0 in two directions. First, it defines how the assistant actually retrieves and reasons over course material at the scale of full courses and their supplementary sources, including licensed textbooks, replacing the earlier placeholder retrieval approach. Second, it defines the assistant's pedagogical behavior across a full course: how it remembers a student, how it paces a tutoring conversation, when it notices a student struggling and reaches out, how it introduces itself, and how it behaves differently and visibly during an assessment. Alongside each new behavior, this version specifies the interface a student and an instructor actually see, since a behavior no one can access is not a feature.

The specification remains written to be adversarial toward its own optimism. Wherever a design choice carries a hidden cost or an unverified assumption, that cost is stated plainly rather than deferred. Everything in version 1.0 that governed compliance, the grading wall, the escalation design, the LTI integration, the platform-adapter seam, is preserved unchanged in this version; version 2.0 adds pedagogy and experience on top of a compliance spine that does not move.

> **Note:** The one-sentence version, updated A single hosted service, built as a standards-based LTI 1.3 tool behind a platform-adapter seam, that answers and tutors students from a retrieval-grounded, multi-source knowledge base (a course's own content plus zero or more approved supplementary sources such as textbooks), remembers each student's progress and difficulties across a full course, proactively checks in when a student is struggling, behaves in a strict, disclosed, guiding-only mode during assessments, auto-scores objective work, drafts applied feedback for a human faculty member to sign, escalates anything it cannot ground or is not authorized to decide, and logs every interaction; instantiated per program with a different persona and guardrail tier, per licensee as an isolated tenant, and per LMS as a thin adapter.

# 1. Problem, Scope, and What This Is Not

## 1.1 The problem being solved

TCI delivers hundreds of courses across three product surfaces: ACI professional development for adult volunteers, AIE professional certificate programs, and TCI University Online for-credit degree courses licensed to universities. Students in all three surfaces need in-context help while they work: clarification of concepts, answers to course-specific questions, formative practice, and feedback. A human being cannot provide that at scale and at all hours across hundreds of courses. An AI assistant can, but only if it is correct, bounded, and defensible. The prior demo assistant proved the concept sells. It proved nothing about whether a trustworthy version can be built, because it answered freely from the model with no authoritative source, no identity, no audit, and no escalation path. Those absences are the entire difference between a demo and a product.

## 1.2 What the assistant is

A hosted, standalone service that students reach from inside their course, not a chatbot bolted onto a webpage.

A tutor and answerer that responds from a specific, authoritative, per-course knowledge source rather than from the model's open-ended knowledge.

An objective-item auto-scorer and an applied-feedback drafter, where the human faculty of record signs every applied grade.

An escalation mechanism that routes anything outside its grounded scope to the responsible human instead of guessing.

A platform: one engine that is instantiated many times, with a swappable persona, a swappable guardrail tier, and a per-course knowledge source.

## 1.3 What the assistant is explicitly not

It is not an autonomous grader of applied or subjective work. It never writes a grade of record for anything beyond auto-scored objective items.

It is not a general-purpose chatbot. If a question falls outside the course's authoritative source, it says so and routes to a human rather than answering from open model knowledge.

It is not a replacement for the faculty of record. In the for-credit context it exists to support the meaningful engagement obligation, never to satisfy it in the human's place.

It is not a homework-completion tool. In graded contexts it tutors toward understanding and refuses to produce the submittable artifact for the student.

It is not a single persona. Nancy, the demo TA, is retired. Persona is a per-program layer, not a fixed identity.

## 1.4 Scope decision: build for all three, ship in sequence

The architecture is designed to serve ACI, AIE, and TCI University Online from the first line of the spec. The rollout is not simultaneous, and treating it as simultaneous is a mistake the spec forbids. The three contexts have different failure costs, and shipping to all three at once produces an assistant that is mediocre everywhere and trusted nowhere. The engine is built to the for-credit standard; each surface relaxes from there.

| Surface | Audience | Cost of a wrong answer | Recommended ship order |
|---|---|---|---|
| TCI University Online | For-credit degree students | Accreditor sees AI contradicting faculty of record on a graded course. Threatens the licensing model. Highest. | Second: it is the design target, but ship after the engine is proven on a lower-stakes surface. |
| AIE certificates | Professional adult learners, no credit | A certificate holder learned something incorrect. Reputational, medium. | Third: broadest content variety, benefits from a hardened engine. |
| ACI | Adult church and conference volunteers | A volunteer is mildly misinformed about ministry practice. Recoverable, lowest. | First: lowest stakes, real users, proves the engine before it touches an accreditation surface. |

> **Note:** Why ACI ships first even though TCI is the design target You design the brakes for the mountain descent and drive them on the flat road first. ACI gives real students, real load, and real edge cases at a stakes level where a mistake is recoverable. Every groundedness and escalation failure ACI surfaces is one you fix before a for-credit transcript is ever involved. Shipping to TCI first means discovering those failures where they can damage the licensing relationship with La Sierra and every downstream client.

# 2. The Layered Instance Model

The assistant is not one program with configuration flags scattered through it. It is four clean layers. Everything shared lives in the engine. Everything that differs by course, program, or context lives in a layer above the engine and is swapped in at instantiation. This separation is what lets one build serve three audiences without becoming a generic lowest-common-denominator tool, and it is what lets a new licensee or a new course be stood up without touching engine code.

| Layer | Varies by | What lives here | Changes when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Engine | Nothing (shared) | API orchestration, context injection, groundedness enforcement, escalation plumbing, audit logging, LTI handshake, auth. Built to for-credit standard. | Rarely. Engine changes affect every instance and are versioned and regression-tested. |
| Knowledge | Per course | The authoritative source of truth the assistant answers from: course content, syllabus, policies, due dates, faculty-of-record identity, approved scope. | Whenever a course's content or syllabus changes. Must be versioned so answers are reproducible. |
| Persona | Per program | Voice and register. ACI warm practitioner; AIE graduate peer; TCI collegial academic. Same engine, different tone. | Rarely. Set once per program, adjusted from student feedback. |
| Guardrail | Per context, tiered | What the assistant will and will not do: academic-integrity posture, privacy posture, faith-content posture, refusal rules. | Per context. A for-credit course inherits the strictest tier automatically. |

## 2.1 Engine layer

The engine is the part that is genuinely hard and genuinely shared. It receives a student message, resolves which course and which student it belongs to from a verified LTI launch, retrieves the relevant slice of that course's authoritative knowledge source, assembles a bounded prompt, calls the model server-side so the API key is never exposed, enforces the groundedness and refusal rules, logs the full exchange, and returns the answer or an escalation. It is built once, to the for-credit standard, and never relaxed. Relaxation happens by loosening the guardrail tier above it, never by weakening the engine.

## 2.2 Knowledge layer

This is the anti-hallucination spine and the layer most likely to be underinvested. The assistant answers from this source, not from the model's open knowledge. When a question falls outside the source, the correct behavior is to say so and escalate, not to improvise a plausible answer. With roughly 96 undergraduate, 113 MBA, the AIE pathways, and the ACI catalog, this is hundreds of distinct knowledge contexts. How they are authored, stored, versioned, and updated when a syllabus changes is a first-class design problem addressed in Section 4, not a detail.

## 2.3 Persona layer

Persona is voice, not capability. The same grounded answer is delivered warmly to an ACI volunteer, as a peer to an AIE professional, and collegially to a for-credit student. Persona never changes what the assistant knows or what it is willing to do. Keeping persona strictly cosmetic prevents a warm ACI tone from accidentally loosening a for-credit integrity rule, which is a real risk if persona and guardrails are entangled.

## 2.4 Guardrail layer

Guardrails are tiered, and a for-credit course inherits the strictest tier without anyone remembering to set it. The three axes that differ most across contexts are academic integrity, privacy, and faith-content handling. The table below is the operative policy, not an illustration.

| Axis | ACI (Tier 1) | AIE (Tier 2) | TCI for-credit (Tier 3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Academic integrity | Tutoring-forward. Few restrictions on producing example artifacts. | Tutors toward mastery; discourages wholesale generation of graded-equivalent work. | Refuses to produce the submittable graded artifact. Tutors to understanding only. Detects and declines do-my-assignment requests. |
| Privacy | General data. Warn before entering personal detail. | General data. No FERPA surface. | FERPA-covered. Student identity handled as protected. Interactions logged and access-controlled. |
| Faith content | Devotional-adjacent, warm, ministry-practical. | Analytical, not devotional. | Analytical. Mission-aligned where the course is, never proselytizing. |
| Out-of-scope | Answer helpfully, note uncertainty. | Answer from source; flag when outside it. | Strict: outside authoritative source means escalate, never improvise. |

## 2.5 The platform-adapter seam

The four layers above describe how the assistant varies by program and context. A fifth structural element describes how it varies by LMS, and it is the difference between a product that runs on one platform and a product that runs on any conformant one. The engine never speaks a specific LMS dialect. It consumes three normalized interfaces, and a thin per-platform adapter translates between the real LMS and those interfaces.

Identity and context: the adapter receives the platform's verified launch and produces a normalized object answering who the student is, which course and offering this is, and what role they hold. The engine never reads raw platform claims.

Grade passback: the adapter exposes a single normalized way to return an objective score to the gradebook, and hides whether the underlying platform calls it Assignment and Grade Services or something else.

Launch and placement: the adapter handles the platform-specific handshake and content placement, so the engine only ever sees a validated, ready session.

This seam is the reason Blackboard support costs almost nothing at the engine level. LTI 1.3 is a shared standard: a tool written once against it is consumable by LearnWorlds, Blackboard, Canvas, Moodle, and D2L alike. The work of adding a platform is writing its adapter and completing its registration and institutional install, not rebuilding the engine. The discipline the seam enforces is negative and strict: no LMS-specific assumption is ever allowed to leak into the engine. The moment identity resolution or course-context logic is written against the shape of one platform's claims, portability is lost and recovering it means refactoring the spine. For that reason the seam is a first-line architectural decision, present before the first adapter is written, not a later accommodation.

> **Note:** LearnWorlds is the first adapter, not the integration The earlier framing treated LearnWorlds as the way the assistant reaches students. That framing is now wrong and is corrected throughout this version. LearnWorlds is the first adapter behind the seam. Blackboard is the second, named because a real client requires it. Neither is the integration; the seam is. Building LearnWorlds-shaped identity into the core and adding Blackboard later is the specific mistake this section exists to prevent.

> **Note:** Designed adapter versus theoretical portability, a distinction this document holds precisely Section 2.5 and Section 6 state that LTI 1.3 makes the engine consumable by LearnWorlds, Blackboard, Canvas, Moodle, and D2L alike, and that claim is true of the standard. It is not the same claim as having a designed, specced adapter for each. As of this version, LearnWorlds, Blackboard, Canvas, and the popout deployment pattern each have their own subsection in Section 6 with platform-specific detail worked through, install path, identity shape, and known constraints. Moodle and D2L are named only because the standard permits them, not because either has been designed, and neither should be presented to a prospective licensee as ready. A reader of this document should treat only the platforms with their own named subsection as actually specified.

# 3. How the Assistant Teaches: Pedagogy, Memory, and Interface

Version 1.0 specified how one answer stays grounded. It did not specify what a full course experience with a student actually looks like across many sessions: how a tutoring conversation is paced, what the assistant remembers about a specific student, when it notices a student struggling and says something first, how it introduces itself, and what any of this actually looks like on screen. This section closes that gap. Each behavior below is specified immediately alongside the interface built for it, since designing the interface before the behavior is settled produces screens for behavior that does not yet exist, and designing the behavior without ever specifying the interface produces a capability no student can actually reach.

> **Note:** Implementation status, added in v2.7 Sections 3.1 and 3.2 below describe design intent, unchanged from v2.6. As of this version, both have been implemented and verified against real, adversarial testing, not merely specified. The diagnose, explain, check, adapt pattern is live in the assistant's system prompt and conversation handling. The student_interaction_history table is live in production with row-level security verified independently against the database, matching the pattern already established for knowledge_documents and knowledge_chunks. Three real findings from that testing are recorded here because each changed the design in a way worth preserving as institutional memory, not just a changelog entry.

## 3.1 The multi-turn tutoring pattern

A single grounded answer is not a tutoring session. A real teaching interaction has shape: it starts with understanding what the student actually needs, tries an explanation, checks whether it landed, and adjusts if it did not, rather than repeating the same explanation more insistently. The assistant follows a defined pattern, not an open-ended conversation with no structure.

Diagnose before explaining. When a student asks a question that could mean several things, or that suggests a misconception rather than a simple information gap, the assistant asks one clarifying or diagnostic question before delivering a full explanation. This mirrors a teacher checking what a student already understands before deciding how to explain something, and it prevents the assistant from re-explaining a concept the student already has and missing the actual point of confusion.

Explain, then check, then adapt. After an explanation, the assistant asks the student to restate the idea, apply it, or confirm understanding in their own words, rather than assuming the explanation worked. If the check reveals the explanation did not land, the assistant tries a different angle or a different example, not the same explanation repeated. Alternate angles and examples must come from within the retrieved course material where the material contains them; where it does not, the assistant may use a general-knowledge illustrative example only for concepts that are themselves general knowledge, widely settled and not specific to this course, and never for course-specific claims, policies, or contested content, consistent with the groundedness controls in Section 3.5. A second example is not worth a groundedness breach. The number of re-explanation attempts before the assistant names that the current approach is not working is not a single flat constant across every kind of confusion; it is a property of the content, drawn from a difficulty or complexity tag in the same assessment and content metadata already specified in Section 4.1. A simple factual recall point and a genuinely difficult conceptual synthesis do not deserve the same patience budget, the way a human tutor's patience scales with what is actually being taught. Two attempts is the reasonable default for content not otherwise tagged, not a ceiling that applies uniformly regardless of what the student is struggling with. Once that budget is reached, the assistant offers a concrete alternative: a different way to think about the topic, a suggestion to revisit a specific part of the course material, or, per Section 3.6's escalation design, a suggestion to reach out to the instructor.

Recognize when a student is going in circles. If the same underlying confusion resurfaces after explanation and adaptation, that is treated as a struggling-student signal (Section 3.3), not as a cue to keep trying variations indefinitely. Persisting past this point risks frustrating the student and wasting the exact moment a human should be looped in.

Warmth without authority. The tutoring pattern is deliberately warm, encouraging, and patient in tone, consistent with the persona layer in Section 2.3. It is equally deliberate about never implying authority the assistant does not have. It never suggests it can grant an extension, waive a requirement, override a policy, or speak for the faculty of record's judgment on a grade or accommodation. This restriction extends to a subtler and more common case: the assistant never characterizes how easy, hard, or fair an upcoming graded assessment will be, since it has no actual knowledge of grading rigor or how an instructor has calibrated a given quiz or exam. A reassurance like this quiz is not too bad is itself an unearned claim of authority, no different in kind from implying it could grant an extension, and it can be flatly wrong in a way that costs the student's trust the moment reality contradicts it. Warmth is a register; authority is a boundary, and the two must never blur into each other, since a student who experiences the assistant as authentically teacher-like is exactly the student most likely to mistake its warmth for institutional authority if the boundary is not held precisely.

> **Note:** Why this needs to be a defined pattern, not just a good prompt An instruction like 'be patient and check understanding' is a tone request, and tone requests drift under pressure, especially when a student is insistent or frustrated. A defined pattern, diagnose, explain, check, adapt, recognize the ceiling, gives the engine concrete session-state to track, how many explanation attempts, whether the last check succeeded, rather than leaving pacing to be reinvented in the moment by the model on every single message.

Interface: the tutoring chat

The chat is the assistant's primary surface and is designed around the pattern above, not around a generic chatbot template. A few specific choices follow directly from the behavior just defined, rather than from visual preference alone.

Visible check-ins, not hidden logic. When the assistant asks a diagnostic or comprehension-check question, it is visually indistinguishable from any other message, since it is a normal part of the conversation, but the underlying session state (explanation attempt count, whether a check has passed) is available to the student as a light, optional signal, for example a small 'still working through this together' indicator rather than a raw counter, so the interaction feels like an ongoing session rather than a series of disconnected questions.

A visible reference to what grounds the answer. Each substantive answer carries a subtle, unobtrusive citation to the part of the course material (or approved supplementary source, Section 4) it rests on, expandable on click rather than inline by default, so the interface stays clean but the provenance principle from Section 3.6 is not just a backend log, it is something the student can actually see and trust.

A calm, uncluttered layout. Single conversation thread per lesson context, generous whitespace, no unnecessary chrome. The chat should read like a conversation with a person, not like a support ticket system. Message input stays simple: a text box, a send action, and the file-attachment affordance explicitly deferred per Section 3.7.

Session continuity is visible, not just remembered silently. If the student left a conversation mid-explanation and returns later, the interface should make it easy to see where the conversation left off, rather than presenting a blank box that gives no sense the assistant has any memory of the prior exchange.

## 3.2 Student interaction history: what the assistant remembers

The multi-turn pattern in Section 3.1 governs a single conversation. Running a full course requires memory that persists across conversations and across the whole course, distinct from the course knowledge source in Section 4, which is about the material, not the student. This is a separate data layer: a per-student, per-course record of what has actually happened.

Concepts the student has asked about, and whether the tutoring pattern's comprehension check on that topic succeeded or not, so a struggle in Module 2 is known context if the same concept resurfaces in Module 4, rather than the assistant starting cold every time.

Gate quiz attempt history from the LMS (pass or fail only, per the hard rule in the LMS Build Plan; this history layer stores the same pass/fail signal, never a score, and inherits that constraint exactly).

A count of tutoring sessions and rough engagement pattern, used only for the struggling-student detection in Section 3.3, never surfaced as a performance metric to the student in a way that could feel like surveillance.

Explicit topics the student has flagged as confusing or asked to revisit, so the assistant can proactively reference them without the student needing to re-explain their own history every time.

> **Note:** This is student data, and it inherits every obligation Section 5 and the LMS Build Plan already established for student data Interaction history is FERPA-relevant in the TCI context exactly as audit logs are, per Section 3.6, and is covered by the same access-control and deletion obligations already specified for audit logging and for the LMS's student data more broadly. It is not a new category of risk requiring new rules; it is new data requiring the same rules already written down elsewhere in this specification and in the LMS Build Plan.

> **Note:** Retention rule: the tutoring memory deletes at course completion; the audit log does not The interaction history is automatically deleted thirty days after a student completes the course, from both the assistant's data layer and any LMS-side mirror of it. The assistant remembers a student for exactly as long as it is teaching them, plus a short buffer for immediate post-completion follow-ups or a reversed completion, and not longer. This rule applies to the tutoring memory only. The audit log in Section 3.7 and the pass or fail completion records in the LMS explicitly survive course completion and are retained per the retention policy, because grade disputes, academic-integrity appeals, and accreditation reviews arise after a course ends, and those records exist to answer exactly those after-the-fact questions. Deleting the working memory at completion is privacy hygiene; deleting the legal record at completion would be destroying evidence at the moment it becomes needed. The two layers were separated in this specification precisely so this distinction could be enforced cleanly.

Interface: making memory visible without making it clinical

A student should feel that the assistant remembers them the way a good teacher does, not that they are being tracked in a dashboard. The interface reflects this distinction deliberately.

No visible scorecard. The student never sees a raw list of their own struggle points as a tracked metric. If the assistant references something from before, it does so conversationally: referencing the concept the student previously found difficult, in the course of teaching, not by surfacing a dashboard of their own weaknesses.

An optional, student-facing progress reflection, not a report card. A student can choose to view a simple, plain-language summary of concepts they have worked through and any they have flagged as needing more attention, framed as a study aid the student controls, not as data collected about them for someone else's use.

Faculty-facing visibility is separate and explicit. A faculty of record can see aggregated, course-level struggle patterns, which concepts many students find hard, not because the assistant surveils individually by default, but because this is exactly the kind of signal that should improve course content over time, consistent with the spec's standing principle that a student's confusion is useful signal for content improvement, not just something to route around silently.

Implementation finding: the evidence-based verdict rule

During testing, the comprehension-check verdict logic needed a decision the original design left implicit. A student who volunteers a correct restatement of a concept, without ever being asked a formal comprehension question, is demonstrating understanding just as validly as a student who answers a posed check correctly. The rule adopted, and now documented in three places in the codebase so it cannot regress into an unexamined assumption, a dated comment in the classifier, the classifier's own system prompt and tool schema, and a pointer comment in the route pointing back to the classifier as the source of truth, is this: a row receives a real true or false verdict whenever the student's response provides clear evidence of understanding or misunderstanding, whether prompted by an explicit check or volunteered unprompted. A row remains null only when there is genuinely no evidence either way. This mirrors how a good human tutor actually works, not every demonstration of understanding follows a formal quiz question, and treating only prompted checks as valid would have discarded real, usable signal about the student's progress.

Implementation finding: the concept-drift bug

Early testing surfaced a real defect worth recording precisely, since the failure mode is subtle and could recur in a different form if the underlying lesson is forgotten. A row's concept field was set when the row was created, at the moment a check was posed, but its pass or fail verdict was filled in later, once the student's next message resolved it. Because the conversation kept moving in between, the assistant had often shifted to a new topic by the time the verdict was recorded, a row could end up labeled with the concept from when the question was asked while its verdict actually reflected a judgment made about a different, later concept. A future struggling-student trigger reading this table would then see a student as failing a concept they had actually mastered, exactly the unreliable signal Section 3.3 explicitly warns against building on. The fix stores the check's own concept at both creation and resolution, so a row's concept and its verdict always describe the same moment in the conversation, verified by re-running the reproduction case and confirming the row correctly re-attached to its actual topic.

Implementation finding: the retraction failure and conversation-aware retrieval

The most serious finding from testing was not a data-quality bug but a live correctness failure reachable by an ordinary student. Retrieval, as originally implemented, built its search query from the literal words of only the newest message, with no awareness of what the conversation had already established. A student who received a correct, grounded answer and then asked a vaguely worded follow-up, phrasing that shared little vocabulary with the original question, could trigger a fresh retrieval that missed the material grounding the earlier answer. The assistant, correctly following its own groundedness rules, then retracted its own correct prior statement, telling the student the course material did not cover something it plainly did, because that turn's retrieval simply never saw it. This is a direct, serious instance of exactly the harm Section 3.5's honesty principle exists to prevent, except turned against a true statement rather than a false one.

The fix makes retrieval conversation-aware: a follow-up's search query is built from recent conversation context, not the newest message in isolation, so a vague follow-up searches for material related to the actual topic under discussion. A second, explicit rule was added to the system prompt as a safeguard independent of retrieval quality: the assistant may not contradict or retract something it already told the student earlier in the same conversation unless newly retrieved material directly conflicts with it. If a follow-up's retrieval comes back thin or off-topic, the assistant says so honestly, for example noting that the material does not go into a specific further detail, without implying the earlier, correct answer was wrong. Both required tests, the retraction reproduction case and a repeat of the original refusal test with material the course genuinely does not cover, were verified passing together, confirming the fix closes the retraction failure without weakening the honest refusal behavior it sits beside.

Implementation finding: the retrieval contamination bug, a second, distinct failure in the same mechanism

The conversation-aware retrieval fix above had a real blind spot of its own, discovered the following session through further adversarial use, not a scripted regression test. Folding recent conversation context into every follow-up's retrieval query assumed the recent turns were still topically relevant. They are not always. In a live reproduction, a student asked a deliberately unrelated, off-topic question mid-conversation, then asked a genuine, on-topic follow-up about course content the material plainly covered. The assistant wrongly claimed the material did not cover it, because the off-topic detour's text, concatenated into the retrieval query alongside the real question, dominated the embedding and pulled retrieval toward unrelated content instead. The failure was measured precisely: the isolated real question alone retrieved the correct material at a similarity of roughly 0.51, while the actual contaminated query retrieved unrelated content at roughly 0.74, with none of the correct material appearing in the top results at all.

The fix is a relevance gate, not a weighting adjustment, since a weighting change would only reduce the severity of contamination rather than eliminate its cause. Before building an augmented retrieval query, a dedicated, fast model call judges whether the new message is an intentional continuation of the recent conversation or a fresh, unrelated question, and only includes prior context in the first case. The distinction that actually matters is intentional continuity, not raw topic similarity, confirmed by a third test case where a student's follow-up explicitly bridged to a different concept in the same breath as referencing the prior one, and was correctly still treated as a follow-up, with retrieval landing on real, verified material despite the topic shift. This relevance check is implemented as a call to the same model powering the assistant, deliberately not a second embedding call, since the embedding provider's rate limit is the actual scarce resource in this system, already requiring queuing and backoff elsewhere, while the language model carries no equivalent constraint here. Verified across three cases together: the original retraction scenario continuing to pass, the exact contamination reproduction now correctly excluding irrelevant context, and the ambiguous bridging case correctly including context and retrieving accurately despite a topic shift.

> **Note:** Why this belongs in the specification, not just a commit log All four findings in this section share a pattern worth naming explicitly: each was caught because a human read real output against a real, specific test case, not because a broad test suite happened to catch it, and the contamination bug specifically was found by continuing to use the product adversarially after the retraction fix, not by assuming one fix closed the whole class of problem. This is the same discipline the specification's compliance sections already assume, verification against a concrete case beats confidence in the design, and it is recorded here so that future extensions of the memory system are tested with the same rigor rather than assumed safe because the design document says so.

Implementation update: course identity is no longer a placeholder, and two new gaps follow from that

The gap described in earlier versions of this specification, a hardcoded course identifier and a client-side, unverified student identifier, has been closed for the join-code deployment path. Real Google authentication via Supabase Auth is now live and verified end to end in a real browser with a real account, and a real course, join-code, and enrollment record now exist in the production database, created through the actual join-code entry screen rather than inserted directly, confirming the full flow works as a real user would experience it, not just as individually tested components. The student identifier flowing into the chat route is now the authenticated user's real identity, not a browser-generated placeholder. This closes the specific gap named here in earlier versions for the join-code path; the LTI-verified path per Section 6.1 remains unbuilt, and is addressed as a named open question in Section 9.

Closing the identity gap surfaced two new, real, and currently undecided gaps that follow directly from it, worth recording precisely rather than letting them sit as unstated assumptions.

The chat route does not yet verify identity or enrollment as strictly as the enrollment route does. The route that creates an enrollment reads the student's verified identity server-side from their authenticated session and never trusts anything the client claims about who they are. The chat route, by contrast, now receives a real, authenticated student identifier, which is a genuine improvement over the placeholder, but it does not yet independently verify that identifier against the session server-side the same way, and it does not yet check that the student is actually enrolled in the course they are chatting about. Both are real hardening gaps, not yet decided as in-scope or out-of-scope, and are named here so the decision is made deliberately rather than discovered later.

Interaction history written before this identity work is now orphaned. Rows in the student interaction history table written under the old placeholder identifier, before real authentication existed, are not tied to any real, verified student and are not currently migrated to one or deleted. This is stale pre-authentication test data rather than a data-integrity failure, but it represents an undecided retention choice, purge it as test noise or leave it as a historical record, that should be resolved deliberately rather than left ambiguous indefinitely.

> **Note:** Retrieval still does not use this history at all Even where interaction history is now tied to real identity, nothing in the retrieval or response generation path currently reads from it. A student's recorded struggle with a concept, or their demonstrated mastery of one, does not yet influence what gets retrieved or how the assistant responds. This is a real, named next step for the memory system, distinct from the identity and enrollment work described above, and it is precisely the kind of capability that would be built on an unreliable foundation if attempted before real identity existed, which is why the identity work was sequenced first.

## 3.3 Struggling-student detection and proactive outreach

Everything so far has the assistant respond to a student who reaches out. Running a full course also means noticing when a student has not reached out, and should have. This is the assistant's one proactive behavior, and it is built narrowly and specifically, because an assistant that reaches out too often, too vaguely, or on a wrong signal reads as intrusive rather than caring, which undermines the entire teacher-like feel this document is trying to build.

Defined triggers only, no open-ended judgment calls. The assistant proactively reaches out only on specific, named conditions: a gate quiz failed more than a defined number of times (recommended: two), a module left untouched as a stated course deadline approaches, or the multi-turn tutoring pattern in Section 3.1 hitting its ceiling, the same underlying confusion resurfacing after the content-appropriate number of explanation attempts per Section 3.1. These are the only triggers. The assistant does not infer struggle from tone, typing speed, or any other soft signal, since those are unreliable and easy to get wrong in ways a student cannot predict or correct for.

The deadline trigger scales its urgency with how far behind the student actually is. A student a day behind a deadline and a student three weeks behind and disengaged are not the same situation, and a single gentle nudge calibrated for the first case reads as tone-deaf, or worse, easy to ignore, for the second. The check-in's framing and its escalation urgency scale with lateness severity, a mild, informational nudge for a student who is slightly behind, moving toward the escalation-to-human path in the next bullet more quickly the longer a student has been disengaged, rather than one flat message regardless of how far behind the student has fallen.

The message is an offer, never a summons. A proactive check-in is framed as the assistant noticing and offering help, never as a notice that the student is being monitored or flagged. It offers a specific next step, revisiting a particular concept, trying the gate quiz differently, reaching out to the instructor, and the student can decline or ignore it without consequence.

Escalation to a human, not just an assistant nudge, past a defined threshold. If the same trigger recurs, for example a second gate quiz failure streak on a different module, or the tutoring ceiling is hit repeatedly across a course, the assistant's proactive behavior converts into the help-request escalation already defined in Section 3.6 and the LMS Build Plan, surfacing to a human, not just repeating itself to the student.

This feature requires architecture the request-response engine does not have, and the spec names it rather than assuming it. The engine as otherwise specified only runs when a student opens a session. Evaluating a deadline-approaching or module-untouched trigger requires a scheduled process that runs outside any conversation, checking trigger conditions against the interaction history and LMS progress data on a defined cadence. This scheduler is a required engine component, not an implied one. The deadline-based trigger additionally applies only where a course actually has stated deadlines; self-paced courses without them simply have no such trigger.

Recognition is not only for difficulty. Every trigger specified above notices a student struggling, and a system that only ever notices difficulty is not actually behaving like a good teacher, it is behaving like a support desk that only opens tickets for problems. A real teacher also notices and encourages a student who is doing well: a strong first attempt on a gate quiz, steady progress through a module without needing help, genuine improvement after a prior struggle. A light, occasional positive acknowledgment, offered with the same restraint as the struggle triggers, rare enough to feel genuine rather than automated praise on every action, is a named trigger alongside the difficulty-based ones, not an afterthought bolted onto a system built only to notice problems.

The delivery channel is an unresolved decision, flagged honestly. A check-in written into the chat only reaches a student who opens the chat, and the student who triggered a deadline-approaching condition is often precisely the student not opening it. The chat message and the dashboard surfacing in Section 10.1 are necessary but not sufficient; an out-of-band channel, most plausibly email sent via the LMS, which owns student contact information, is likely required for the feature's core use case to reach its target. Which channel, and its opt-out mechanics, is a named open question in Section 9, deliberately not decided unilaterally here.

> **Note:** The risk this design is built against A proactive AI teaching assistant sits close to a real harm if built carelessly: a student experiencing generalized difficulty could receive a stream of automated check-ins that feel like pressure rather than support, or could come to feel watched rather than taught. The defined-trigger and offer-not-summons rules exist specifically to prevent that. If this feature is ever expanded, any new trigger must meet the same bar: specific, rare enough to feel meaningful when it happens, and framed as an offer.

Interface: the check-in

A proactive check-in appears as a message from the assistant inside the normal chat surface, not as a separate notification system, since a distinct alert channel would read as more clinical and more surveillance-like than a normal conversational message. It is visually identical to any other assistant message. If the trigger has escalated to a human per the rule above, a separate, clearly-labeled message tells the student a check-in has been shared with their instructor, so nothing happens to the student's data silently.

## 3.4 Onboarding: the first conversation

The first time a student opens the assistant in a course, expectations get set that shape every interaction after. This is a short, defined first-contact sequence, not left to whatever the model improvises on a first message.

The assistant introduces itself in one or two sentences: what it is, and that it is a teaching aid grounded in this specific course's material, not a general-purpose AI.

It states, plainly and once, what it can and cannot do: it can explain, tutor, and answer questions from the course material; it cannot change deadlines, override policy, or speak for the instructor's judgment on a grade.

It previews, honestly and briefly, that during a graded assessment it works differently, in a guiding-only mode, disclosed at the time, per Section 3.5, so a student is not caught off guard the first time they encounter it during a real quiz.

It invites the first real question, rather than waiting passively for the student to guess what to ask a tool they have never used before.

This sequence appears once per course per student, not once per session, and is reachable again afterward from a simple help or about affordance in the interface, for a student who wants to revisit it.

Interface: first-run experience

A short, dismissible introductory panel appears the first time the chat opens in a new course, containing the four points above in plain language, not a lengthy terms-of-use style document. It requires only a single acknowledgment to proceed, and it never reappears automatically once dismissed, though it remains available from a persistent, unobtrusive help icon in the chat interface.

## 3.5 Source-pinning and groundedness

This is roughly eighty percent of the trust problem. The assistant must answer from the course's authoritative knowledge source and must refuse to answer confidently outside it. The mechanism is retrieval-grounded generation: the engine retrieves the relevant material from the course's knowledge source, supplies it to the model as the basis for the answer, and instructs the model to answer only from that material and to declare when the material does not cover the question. Groundedness is enforced, not merely requested, through three reinforcing controls. Section 4 defines how retrieval itself works at the scale of full courses and supplementary sources such as textbooks; this section defines the controls that apply regardless of retrieval mechanism.

Retrieval scoping: the model is given the relevant slice of the authoritative source and told that this is its permitted basis. Open-ended world knowledge is treated as unreliable for course-specific claims.

Refusal-on-miss: when retrieval returns nothing relevant, the engine does not fall through to an open answer. It returns an honest miss and offers escalation. A confident answer with no retrieved support is the primary failure mode being designed against.

Provenance surfacing: where appropriate, the answer indicates which part of the course material it rests on, so a student and later a reviewer can trace it. This also creates natural pressure against fabrication, and per Section 3.1's interface design, this provenance is visible to the student, not just logged in the backend.

Student-facing correction: every substantive answer carries a simple, low-friction way for the student to flag it as wrong or off, not only a backend logging mechanism but a visible option the student can act on in the moment. Given how much of this document is built around the possibility of the assistant being ungrounded, stale, or mistaken, a system with no way for the person actually receiving a bad answer to say so is missing the most direct correction signal available. A flagged answer is logged with the same fidelity as any other interaction per Section 3.7, surfaces in the faculty struggle-pattern view per Section 3.2 as a distinct category from ordinary confusion, and, where the flag suggests the knowledge source itself is wrong or stale rather than the student being confused, feeds directly into the versioning and update discipline in Section 4.5.

> **Note:** The test that matters Ask the assistant something the course genuinely does not cover. A trustworthy build says it does not know and offers to route the question to the instructor. The demo build, and any build that answers from open model knowledge, produces a fluent and possibly wrong answer. That single behavioral difference is the line between a product and a liability.

## 3.6 Escalation to a human

Escalation is what makes the assistant defensible under accreditation review, because it is the concrete mechanism by which the human faculty of record remains in the loop. The assistant escalates, rather than answers, in a defined set of situations, and the routing target is the responsible human for that course, not a generic support queue.

The question falls outside the authoritative knowledge source.

The question is a matter of faculty judgment or policy discretion the assistant is not authorized to decide, such as a deadline extension, a grade dispute, or an accommodation.

The interaction shows signs of distress, crisis, or a wellbeing concern, which routes to the appropriate human channel immediately and is never handled by the assistant alone.

The student explicitly asks for a human.

The assistant detects a likely academic-integrity situation in a graded context and needs a human decision rather than an automated one.

The struggling-student threshold in Section 3.3 recurs past its defined escalation point.

## 3.7 Audit logging

In a for-credit context you will eventually have to answer the question, what did the AI tell my student, and if you cannot, you are exposed. Every interaction is logged with enough fidelity to reconstruct it: the student identity from the verified launch, the course and instance, the student input, the retrieved material the answer rested on, the answer returned, any escalation raised, and the timestamp. Logs are access-controlled and retained per policy, and in the TCI context they are treated as FERPA-covered records. This is infrastructure with legal weight, not analytics. The student interaction history in Section 3.2 is a distinct data layer from this log; the log is a record of what happened, the history is a working model of the student's progress the assistant actively uses, but both inherit the same access-control and FERPA obligations.

One deliberate exception to full reconstruction fidelity: audit entries for gate quiz interactions record the attempt event and its pass or fail outcome only, never per-item correctness. Logging which individual questions a student answered correctly would make a percentage trivially recomputable from the log, creating a de facto score record that collides with the hard rule, established in the LMS Build Plan and inherited here, that no score for a completion gate exists anywhere in any system. The no-score rule binds the log with the same force it binds the schema; a rule the log can reconstruct around is not a rule.

A second cross-system obligation: the student interaction history and the audit logs held by this assistant service must be deletable through the same deletion flow the LMS Build Plan's hardening phase defines. Concretely, the assistant service exposes a deletion endpoint for a given student identity, and the LMS's admin deletion flow is required to call it, so a data-deletion request fulfilled in the LMS cannot silently leave that student's interaction record alive in the assistant's own data layer. A deletion that spans two systems and completes in only one is a FERPA deletion failure, and this obligation is stated in both documents so neither can assume the other owns it.

## 3.8 Assessment mode: a guarding, not a guess

The assistant must never help a student answer a live quiz or exam question directly. The naive approach to this problem is to have the assistant try to detect, from conversation alone, whether a question sounds like a test question. That approach is unreliable by construction: homework questions and assessment questions are often phrased identically, and a false negative here produces exactly the failure being guarded against. Assessment mode is therefore not a judgment the assistant makes. It is a context flag the system already knows and truthfully reports.

The source of truth is the LMS, not the model's inference. When a student is inside a gate quiz on the TCI-controlled LMS (per the LMS Build Plan's Phase 2), the system already knows this because that is literally what page or session the student is in. This is the same normalized context object already described in Section 2.5 and Section 6.2, with assessment context added as a field on it: true or false, reported by the system, never inferred by the assistant.

Blackboard-hosted exams are a defined future capability, not assumed to work today. This same mechanism only functions for a Blackboard exam if the assistant is deliberately launched from inside that specific assessment experience via its own LTI placement, exactly as it would be embedded in a lesson. Until that placement is deliberately built, the safer and simpler default is that the assistant is not embedded inside Blackboard exam pages at all, so there is no ambiguous state, and no unreliable inference is ever required.

While the flag is on, the assistant is strictly Socratic, defined precisely, not just described as a tone. In assessment mode the assistant may ask the student to explain their own reasoning, may point to which general concept or which part of the course material is relevant, and may ask clarifying questions about what specifically is confusing. It may never affirm or deny a specific proposed answer, never narrow down among multiple-choice options even by process of elimination, and never restate or rephrase the question's content in a way that reveals which detail matters. This is written as a specific, testable behavior, whether the assistant ever confirms or eliminates an answer choice, rather than a tone instruction a model might not hold consistently under a well-phrased but manipulative student prompt.

The restriction is disclosed to the student explicitly, every time. When assessment mode activates, the assistant states plainly that it is now in a guided mode because this is a graded assessment, and that it will not confirm or provide answers until the assessment is submitted. This is consistent with the honesty principle already governing escalation in Section 3.6: say what is actually true, rather than silently behave differently and let the student wonder why.

The restriction holds regardless of how the student reframes the question. A student may attempt to route around the restriction by rephrasing a quiz question as hypothetical, as a general question unrelated to the quiz, or as something asked purely out of curiosity while the flag is active. The restriction applies to all questions for the full duration the assessment-mode flag is on, without exception for framing. Any exception here creates exactly the seam a motivated student would use to defeat the rule, since any specific quiz question can be rephrased as a general one with minimal effort. A rule that admits no framing-based exception has no such seam.

Enforcement is structural, not behavioral: in assessment mode, the retrieval layer withholds answer-bearing content. The runtime model for this assistant is a fixed, cost-efficient model, and a behavioral rule the model must hold on every message against a persistent, manipulative student is not a sufficient defense on its own. The robust defense is to starve the model rather than discipline it: while the assessment-mode flag is active, the retrieval step excludes the chunks tagged answer-bearing per Section 4.1, supplying only concept-level material, or nothing, as the grounding basis. A model that was never shown the answer cannot leak it, no matter how well the student prompts. The behavioral Socratic rule remains in force on top of this, but it is the second line of defense, not the first. This enforcement is only as good as the human tagging behind it, per Section 4.1; it is not a fully automated guarantee, and course builders bear real responsibility for tagging accurately, particularly where a course's own worked example doubles as the basis for an assessed question.

Supplementary media search is disabled while the flag is active. Section 4.4's capability to surface an explanatory video is off in assessment mode without exception, since a video teaching the assessed concept mid-quiz is an answer leak through a side door, defeating the Socratic restriction while technically never having the assistant state an answer itself. Section 4.4 carries the reciprocal statement of this rule.

> **Note:** Why this is a guardrail axis, not a persona setting Assessment mode belongs alongside academic integrity, privacy, and faith content as a fourth axis in the guardrail layer defined in Section 2.4, because it is a categorical behavior change triggered by verified context, exactly like the other three axes, not a stylistic adjustment. A for-credit course's Tier 3 guardrail already refuses to produce a submittable graded artifact; assessment mode is the same discipline applied specifically to live assessment interactions.

Interface: making the mode change unmistakable

A silent behavior change here would be both confusing and easy to miss, so assessment mode is made visually explicit, not left to the student to infer from the assistant's wording alone.

A persistent visual state change. While assessment mode is active, the chat interface displays a clear, simple indicator, a labeled banner or a distinct border treatment, communicating that the assistant is in guided mode for this assessment. This is not a warning styled to alarm the student; it is a calm, factual state indicator, consistent with the disclosure being an honest feature rather than a punitive one.

The indicator persists for the full duration of the flag, not just the first message. A student who scrolls back or returns to the conversation mid-assessment should still see the mode clearly, not just recall it from an earlier message that has scrolled out of view.

A distinct, calmer transition back to normal mode. When the assessment ends and the flag clears, the interface returns to its normal appearance and the assistant can, if relevant, offer to discuss the material now that the assessment is complete, reinforcing that the restriction was specific to the assessment, not a general demotion of the assistant's helpfulness.

# 4. The Knowledge Source: Retrieval, Multi-Source Content, and Supplementary Media

Version 1.0 left the retrieval mechanism undefined, correctly, since it depended on a decision this version now makes. A course's authoritative material is not always small enough to hand to the model in full. TCI's own course content (5,000 to 9,500 words per course, organized into modules) comfortably fits in a single model call. A licensed textbook, sometimes multiple per course, sometimes none, does not; textbooks commonly run into the hundreds of thousands of words. This section defines a single retrieval architecture that handles both, rather than two separate systems for two data shapes, since textbook attachment varies course to course in a way that would make a hard split between systems a source of ongoing special-casing rather than a simplification.

## 4.1 A course's knowledge source is a collection, not a single document

Every course's authoritative material is modeled as a collection of one or more documents. TCI's own authored module and chapter content is always present as one or more documents in the collection. Zero, one, or several external sources, most commonly a licensed textbook, may also be attached to the same collection. A source being present or absent is a normal state of the model, not a special case requiring different logic; the retrieval pipeline treats every document in a course's collection identically at the mechanical level.

Retrieval is embeddings-based (retrieval-augmented generation) across the full collection: every document is chunked, embedded, and stored so a student's question can be matched semantically against the most relevant passages, regardless of which document, or which module, chapter, or textbook page, they come from.

TCI's own authored content is ranked with priority over supplementary sources when both are relevant to a question, since it is the material directly aligned to this specific course's objectives and assessments, and a supplementary textbook is depth and context around it, not a competing authority.

Provenance is tracked per chunk, not just per course, so an answer's citation (Section 3.1's interface, Section 3.5's groundedness control) can tell a student and a reviewer not just that an answer is grounded, but specifically whether it rests on TCI's own material or on a supplementary source, which matters for both instructional trust and copyright accountability.

Chunks are tagged answer-bearing where they contain the specific content a graded assessment tests, as part of the assessment metadata already required by this section, and this tagging is a required human step at course-build time, not an inference the retrieval system performs automatically. The boundary between a chunk that teaches a concept and a chunk that also happens to contain the answer to an assessed question is not always clean, for example when a course's own worked example is the same example a quiz question is built from, and no automated process should be trusted to draw that line reliably. A faculty member or course builder marks these chunks explicitly when assessment items are authored, and Section 3.8's retrieval-starvation mechanism withholds exactly the chunks tagged this way, nothing more and nothing less.

## 4.2 Textbook licensing is a prerequisite, not an afterthought

Before any external textbook or supplementary document is embedded into a course's knowledge source, its licensing terms must be confirmed to permit this specific use: ingestion into a vector store and the serving of AI-generated answers derived from its content. This is a legal and business review, not a technical step, and it must happen before ingestion, not be discovered as a problem afterward across however many courses already used that textbook. A course missing this confirmation does not get its textbook ingested until the confirmation exists.

## 4.3 A missing textbook is a flag for a human, never a live substitution

Faculty do not always upload a course's textbook, and the assistant must never respond to that gap by reaching out to the open web on its own initiative to improvise a substitute. Doing so would reopen the exact groundedness failure Section 3.5 exists to prevent: an unverified, unversioned, unreviewed source entering a for-credit answer with no one having approved it, potentially the wrong edition, a summary site, or simply an inaccurate result. This gap is instead a content-completeness workflow.

An admin-facing tool can search for and suggest candidate textbook sources, by title, edition, and ISBN, when a course's collection has no textbook attached.

Candidates are presented for a human, the faculty of record or a course admin, to review and approve before anything is ingested, using the same preview-before-publish discipline already established for the LMS's zip importer.

The live, student-facing assistant never performs this search itself and never substitutes a self-selected source at answer time. If no textbook is attached and a question genuinely requires one, the assistant behaves exactly as Section 3.5's refusal-on-miss control specifies: it says so honestly and offers escalation, rather than improvising.

> **Note:** Implementation update, v2.7: answer_bearing is now enforced, not just tagged A real, reproduced academic-integrity incident occurred during testing: a graded assignment prompt, tagged answer_bearing in the schema per this section's design, was retrieved for an ordinary conceptual question, and the assistant performed the assignment's actual analytical task live in the response, reusing the assignment's specific scenario, closely approximating a worked answer a student could have submitted directly. This was not a retrieval defect; retrieval correctly found topically relevant content. The defect was that tagging alone did nothing, the answer_bearing column existed and was populated correctly on twenty-five real chunks, but match_knowledge_chunks never filtered on it, so the tag was inert. The fix adds the same unconditional exclusion already enforced for license_confirmed, directly in the SQL function: any chunk with answer_bearing set to true is excluded from retrieval results entirely, full exclusion, no exceptions, and no assignment-specific retrieval path yet, since a reliable way to distinguish a student legitimately asking about their own assignment from a general question that happens to retrieve the same content has not been designed. The fix was verified across five independent test runs of the exact reproduction case, not one, since the original leak itself only surfaced on one of two otherwise-identical runs, meaning model sampling variance, not retrieval inconsistency, was driving the failure, and a single clean re-test would not have been sufficient evidence of a fix.

A related finding during the same review: several graded discussion-question prompts in the source material shared no explicit numbered label with the formally graded Activities, but carried the same structural shape, a specific scenario paired with a required analytical task. These were tagged answer_bearing as well, on the reasoning that the risk this guardrail exists to prevent is a function of structure, a scenario the model could complete on the student's behalf, not of whether a prompt happens to carry a formal label or a stated word count.

> **Note:** A second, distinct retrieval-layer flag, assessment_scope, added in v3.6. answer_bearing withholds chunks whose content is a graded prompt the model could complete on a student's behalf. A separate hazard exists even where no prompt is at risk of being completed: chunks whose content is a scope statement, an itemized list of topics an upcoming graded assessment covers, are freely groundable, factual course content by every other measure, but reciting one on request tells a student what is on their assessment, which is a different failure mode from answer completion and needs its own flag rather than an extension of answer_bearing's meaning. assessment_scope is enforced the same way, unconditionally, in the retrieval SQL function itself, not as a prompt instruction, and identification is intentionally narrow: a chunk qualifies only when it begins with the scope-statement marker, not merely contains one anywhere in its text, since ingest chunking can split a section header away from unrelated material that happens to end with the same words, and a content-match rule would silently withhold real teaching content along with genuine scope statements. The two flags are kept independent rather than merged so that a future operator view can report which chunks were withheld and for which of the two distinct reasons.

## 4.4 Supplementary media search: a narrow, separate capability

Distinct from the knowledge source itself, the assistant may search for and surface a supplementary explanatory resource, most commonly a video, when a student has not understood something despite the tutoring pattern in Section 3.1 reaching its explanation ceiling. This is not a retrieval enhancement to the knowledge source; it is a bounded, separate capability with its own controls, because live web search is a fundamentally different risk profile from retrieval over an approved, versioned collection.

Triggered only after a grounded answer already exists. The assistant never uses supplementary search as a first response or as a substitute for answering from the approved source. It activates only once the assistant has already given a grounded answer per Section 3.5 and the tutoring pattern's explanation ceiling has been reached without success.

Scoped to reputable video platforms, not the open web. Search is restricted to known, reputable video platforms, principally YouTube, rather than an unrestricted web crawl that could surface a low-quality, inaccurate, or inappropriate result. This restriction is a cheap, high-value control compared to the alternative of trusting open-web result quality.

The assistant surfaces the resource without vouching for its content beyond topic match. Choosing and describing a specific video is itself an implicit claim of relevance and quality the assistant cannot fully verify. The assistant states this honestly: it presents the resource as covering the topic, explicitly notes that it has not verified everything in the video against the course material, and invites the student to flag anything that seems to conflict with what the course has taught. This mirrors the honesty principle already governing escalation and assessment-mode disclosure: state what is actually known, never imply a confidence that has not been earned.

Every use is logged. Same fidelity as any other interaction per Section 3.7, so if a surfaced resource later turns out to be inaccurate or inappropriate, every student it was shown to can be identified and, if needed, informed.

Disabled entirely while assessment mode is active. Per Section 3.8, this capability is off without exception for the full duration of an assessment-mode flag, since surfacing a video that teaches the assessed concept mid-quiz would leak the answer through a side door while the Socratic restriction held perfectly in the assistant's own words.

> **Note:** This is a media-discovery feature, not a factual-retrieval feature The substance of any answer still comes only from the approved knowledge source, per Section 3.5. Supplementary search only ever adds a second way of presenting an explanation the assistant has already grounded correctly; it is never the source of a new factual claim. If a supplementary video appears to contradict the course's own material, the course's own material governs, and the assistant says so.

Interface: supplementary resources and the admin content tools

Two distinct interfaces follow from this section, one student-facing and one admin-facing, and they should not be conflated.

Student-facing: a clearly distinguished supplementary card, not an inline link. A surfaced video appears as a visually distinct card in the chat, separate from the assistant's own grounded text, carrying a brief, honest label such as this may help, not verified against our course material in full, so a student can never mistake supplementary media for the assistant's own authoritative answer.

Admin-facing: a knowledge-source management view. An admin or faculty view lists each course's attached sources, TCI's own content plus any supplementary documents, shows which are missing a confirmed license or a textbook entirely, and surfaces the search-and-approve workflow from Section 4.3 directly in this view, so a faculty member sees at a glance which of their courses have a content gap and can resolve it in the same place, rather than this being a hidden backend process with no visible home in the product.

## 4.5 Versioning is not optional

A due date changes. A policy is revised. A module is rewritten. If the assistant answers from a stale source, it will confidently tell a student the wrong deadline, which in a for-credit course is a real harm. The knowledge source is therefore versioned, each answer records which version and which documents it rested on, and updating a course's content re-embeds the changed material through a defined step rather than by silent, untracked editing. The audit log's reference to source version is what makes a past answer explicable even after the course has changed. This applies identically whether the changed document is TCI's own content or a supplementary source.

## 4.6 The deduplication discipline applies here too

The existing TCI rule that new catalog courses are deduplicated against the full existing catalog before build has an analogue here. Knowledge sources must be built from the authoritative current version of a course. The rebuild states of the MBA and School of Education catalogs mean their sources cannot be finalized until those rebuilds settle. Standing up the assistant on a catalog that is mid-rebuild would pin it to content that is about to change. ACI shipping first is partly a consequence of this: its content is stable, while the for-credit catalogs are not yet.

# 5. Assessment and Grading: The Compliance Wall

This is the feature most likely to be built wrong, because the naive version is easy and the correct version is bounded. The governing principle is fixed and non-negotiable, and it comes from the assurance-of-learning obligation itself: AI auto-scoring of outcome-tagged objective items is compliant; final applied components require the faculty of record's judgment to satisfy meaningful engagement. The assistant is built to that line and never crosses it.

| Assessment type | What the assistant does | What writes the grade of record |
|---|---|---|
| Objective items (multiple choice, matching, outcome-tagged) | Auto-scores. May pass the score back to the LMS gradebook. | The assistant, within its authorized objective-scoring scope only. |
| Applied and subjective work (essays, projects, capstone components) | Drafts rubric-aligned feedback and surfaces the relevant outcome tags. Holds the draft for review. | The faculty of record, who reviews, edits, and signs. Always the human. |
| Anything ambiguous | Escalates to the faculty of record rather than deciding. | The faculty of record. |

> **Note:** The platform gives you the gun; the spec forbids pulling the trigger LearnWorlds LTI 1.3 supports automated score passback to the gradebook via Assignment and Grade Services. That means the assistant could technically write applied grades directly. It must not. Score passback is enabled only for auto-scored objective items. Applied feedback is drafted and held for faculty signature and is never passed back autonomously. If an accreditation reviewer finds an AI writing applied grades of record in a product licensed under the university's own accreditation, the finding does not just fail the feature, it endangers the license.

A second, quieter benefit of this boundary: it converts the assistant from a compliance risk into a compliance asset. A drafted, faculty-signed feedback workflow with outcome tags surfaced for the human actually strengthens the assurance-of-learning story, because it makes the loop-closing visible and consistent while keeping the human judgment that the standard requires. The boundary is not a limitation to apologize for. It is the feature.

# 6. Integration: One Standard, Many Platforms

The assistant is embedded inside the student's course. The naive approach is a raw iframe embed, and it is wrong for the for-credit context for a specific and disqualifying reason: a raw embed can pass variables like the student's name and course id, but those variables are unverified and trivially spoofable. In a FERPA context, an unverified claim of student identity is worthless. The correct approach is LTI 1.3, and its decisive advantage beyond security is portability. LTI 1.3 is a shared standard from the 1EdTech group. A tool written once against it is consumable by any conformant platform, including LearnWorlds, Blackboard Learn, Canvas, Moodle, and D2L. The assistant is therefore built once as an LTI 1.3 tool and reaches each platform through its adapter behind the seam of Section 2.5.

## 6.1 What LTI 1.3 gives that an iframe does not

Verified identity and context. The launch passes a signed token carrying verified claims about who the student is and which course they are in, and the assistant verifies the signature against the platform's published keys. This is the identity problem solved by a standard rather than invented.

Secure launch handshake. Single-sign-on-style launch, so the student does not authenticate separately and the assistant still knows who they are for logging and personalization.

Structured score passback. The Assignment and Grade Services component can return scores to the gradebook, used here only for objective items per Section 5.

Cross-platform portability. The same conformant tool runs on every LTI 1.3 platform. Adding a platform is writing an adapter and completing its registration, not rebuilding the engine.

## 6.2 Identity is normalized, not platform-shaped

Different platforms identify users differently. LearnWorlds keys users by email address. Blackboard identifies by a subject claim with email as a configurable alternative, alongside its own deployment and context identifiers. If the engine resolves student or course identity by reading the shape of one platform's claims, it breaks on the next platform. The adapter for each platform therefore translates that platform's specific claims into the single normalized identity object the engine consumes, and the engine remains ignorant of which platform it is running on. This is the concrete reason the platform-adapter seam is a first-line decision rather than a later accommodation.

## 6.3 Multi-tenancy and per-platform registration

Every licensee institution is a separate tenant with isolated logs and data, and this holds across platforms. On LearnWorlds an LTI integration is configured per school, so La Sierra, Burman, and PUC each require their own deployment and credentials. On Blackboard the tool is registered once in the vendor developer portal and then installed per institution by that institution's administrator. In both cases one licensee's student data must never be reachable from another's, so the engine is tenant-aware from the first line. A tenant is a set of instances with isolated data; the platform it runs on is an adapter detail beneath that isolation.

## 6.4 Blackboard deployment is an institutional process, not a configuration step

Blackboard support is nearly free at the engine level and expensive at the institutional level, and the spec is honest about where the cost actually sits. On LearnWorlds, where TCI or the licensee controls the school, standing up the tool is a configuration task. On Blackboard it is a governed institutional process with a real lead time and a real gate, and none of it can be initiated by TCI as an outside vendor. The request must come from a faculty or staff member inside the institution, and the institution's central IT installs and configures the tool on their Learn instance.

| Fact about Blackboard deployment | Consequence for TCI |
|---|---|
| The request must be initiated by institutional faculty or staff, never by the outside vendor. | TCI needs an internal champion at the client to start the process. This is a relationship dependency, not an engineering one. |
| Installation runs 4 to 8 weeks, sometimes 1 to 3 months, from request to live. | The Blackboard client's go-live date is set by their IT calendar, not TCI's build schedule. Plan the deal timeline around it. |
| Review covers functionality, accessibility, security, FERPA, and legal. | TCI must be able to answer all five, in documents, before the tool is approved. |
| Institutions increasingly require a HECVAT, a SOC 2 Type 2 report, a VPAT, and a signed Data Processing Agreement. | These are business deliverables that take months and money to produce. The absence of a SOC 2 Type 2 in particular can stall an otherwise-won deal. |
| Under FERPA the tool operates as a school official performing an outsourced function. | TCI must be contractually willing to accept school-official obligations and data-processing terms, not just technically capable. |

> **Note:** The compliance package is the real Blackboard build For a real Blackboard client, the adapter is a few weeks of engineering. The trust package, a HECVAT, a SOC 2 Type 2 audit, a VPAT, and a signed data processing agreement, is the actual gating deliverable and it is measured in months and dollars. SOC 2 Type 2 requires an independent auditor observing your controls over a monitoring period, so it cannot be produced on demand when a client asks. This belongs in the go-to-market plan and the deal timeline, exactly like the credit-versus-non-credit question already flagged for Burman. Resolve what the client's IT will require before the meeting, not during the build.

## 6.5 The things to verify before building each adapter

> **Note:** Verify, do not assume For LearnWorlds: confirm against the specific school plan tier that LTI 1.3 consumer support and Assignment and Grade Services score passback are included, and that the per-school configuration matches the tenant model. For Blackboard: confirm the specific client institution's LTI review requirements and required compliance artifacts, their installation lead time against your target go-live, and who the internal requester will be. Plan-tier gating and institution-specific review rubrics are exactly the details that differ from the general documentation, and getting either wrong wastes a build or stalls a deal.

## 6.6 The gated front-end pattern

While a full Blackboard adapter is pending, or as a permanent shape for some course types, a course can legitimately run its content, its formative work, and the assistant on a separate LMS that TCI controls directly, typically with simple Google-account login, while the graded assessments of record stay in Blackboard. This is not a workaround. It is the same best-of-breed separation LTI itself is built for, teaching surface and system of record living in different places, and it lets a for-credit course benefit from the assistant well before any institutional Blackboard install completes.

The pattern splits the course cleanly. The TCI-controlled LMS carries the instructional content, the assistant in full tutoring mode, and formative practice items, including pass or fail completion quizzes that gate progression through the material. Blackboard carries the actual exams and major assignments that the faculty of record evaluates and that constitute the grade of record. A student who finishes the material on the TCI LMS becomes eligible to sit the Blackboard assessments; the two systems are connected by eligibility, not by shared grade data.

> **Note:** Hard rule: the completion gate may never carry grade weight A pass or fail completion result from the TCI-controlled LMS may condition access to the graded assessments in Blackboard. It may never itself be worth any percentage of the course grade, in any format, regardless of how confidently it is labeled a certificate of completion. The reasoning is the same reasoning behind the Section 5 grading wall, extended to a second surface. A completion gate is automated logic with no faculty-of-record judgment in its chain, so if it carried grade weight it would let an unreviewed component determine part of a student's outcome in a for-credit course, which is the specific failure the assurance-of-learning standard exists to prevent. It also splits the system of record: any grade weight living outside Blackboard becomes a component an accreditor, a registrar, or a FERPA records request cannot see or reconstruct under institutional authority. The safe sentence for a syllabus is 'you must complete the pre-work modules to access the graded assessments.' The unsafe sentence, never to be written, is any version of 'the pre-work modules are worth some percentage of your grade.' If a component is ever intended to carry grade weight, it requires a faculty-of-record signature somewhere in its chain, exactly as in Section 5, and a pass or fail completion gate has no such signature by design.

Because the completion gate carries no grade weight, its policy questions are operational rather than compliance questions. A stated retry policy, unlimited attempts on the gate quiz is the safest and most defensible default since the stakes are sequencing rather than scoring, and a defined human escalation path for a student who remains stuck, consistent with Section 3.2, are the two decisions to make before launch rather than after a support incident.

## 6.7 Popout deployment: the chat window on its own monitor, still tied to the live session

A student may want the chat window open in its own browser tab, on a second monitor, separate from the rest of the course page. This is simpler than a detached standalone deployment would be, and it is worth stating plainly why: rather than building a session that exists apart from the LMS and then solving the problems that separation creates, the popout tab is a second window onto the exact same live session, never a session of its own. There is no new identity handoff, no separate authentication, and no synchronization problem to solve, because there is nothing running independently that would need to be kept in sync.

Mechanism: an expand affordance on the embedded chat opens a new tab carrying the current session forward. The student is already authenticated into the LMS to have reached the chat at all. Expanding it passes the current session's identity and course-context object to a new tab via a signed token in the URL, rather than requiring a fresh login. The popout is not a new session; it is the same session rendered in a second window. This token is single-use and expires within a small fixed window, on the order of sixty seconds, after which it is invalid regardless of whether it was used. A token sitting in a URL can end up in browser history, referrer headers, or a shared screenshot, so it is treated as a credential that must be spent almost immediately or not at all, never as a durable session identifier. Once the popout tab has exchanged the token for its own live connection to the session, the token itself has no further value even if it were somehow captured afterward.

Assessment mode carries over automatically, because there is nothing separate to bypass. The detached standalone design this section previously specified required a workaround for assessment mode, since a truly independent session had no inherent way to know an assessment was live. The popout has no such gap: it reflects the same live state as the embedded chat, because it is drawing from the same session, not a separate one polling for a stale snapshot of it. If the LMS session is in assessment mode, the popout is in assessment mode, with no additional mechanism required.

The popout closes when its parent session ends. If the LMS tab or the underlying session is closed or times out, the popout closes with it. The popout's entire existence is contingent on the live session it extends; it is deliberately not designed to survive independently, since doing so would quietly reintroduce the detached-session problem this design otherwise avoids entirely.

> **Note:** Why this replaces, rather than supplements, a detached standalone mode A previous version of this section specified a fully detached standalone deployment, reachable independent of any LMS session, which required a periodic live check for an open assessment and a lock state during one, since a genuinely separate session had no other way to know. The popout design solves the same underlying request, the assistant open on its own monitor, without reopening that problem at all, because it is never actually separate from the session it came from. Where a simpler design removes a class of problem rather than solving it more cleverly, the simpler design is preferred, consistent with this document's standing pattern of closing a gap rather than building a clever way to manage it.

One consequence of the popout being a live extension of the same session, rather than a separate one: a proactive check-in per Section 3.3, or an escalation notice, is delivered to whichever surface is currently the active view for that session, the embedded chat or the popout, never both. If both surfaces are open simultaneously, the message appears once, in the surface the student most recently interacted with, since a duplicated message across two windows of what the student experiences as one conversation would read as a glitch rather than a feature, and would undermine the calm, settled tone this document's design principles in Section 10.2 call for.

## 6.8 Canvas: a fourth named adapter, and an easier install than Blackboard

Canvas supports LTI 1.3 and LTI Advantage Complete, all three Advantage services, Assignment and Grade Services, Names and Role Provisioning, and Deep Linking, so this is the same standard already built against for LearnWorlds and Blackboard, not new engine work. Canvas earns its own named subsection rather than resting on the general portability claim in Section 2.5, per the designed-versus-theoretical distinction stated there, because its install path has a real, specific difference from Blackboard worth documenting precisely rather than assuming the two are equivalent.

Configuration is self-service through Developer Keys, not a multi-week institutional gate. Canvas stores tool configuration through Developer Keys: an administrator with sufficient permissions creates a key, either manually or by supplying a JSON configuration, enables the specific LTI Advantage Services scopes the tool needs, and deploys it to an account, a sub-account, or an individual course. This is an admin-console action inside Canvas, architecturally closer to how LearnWorlds is configured than to Blackboard's admin-initiated, multi-week, HECVAT-and-SOC-2-gated institutional review. A licensee's Canvas administrator can plausibly stand up the integration in a single session, not a quarter.

Assessment-mode context is available at launch, not just at score passback. Canvas sends specific parameters when a student accesses a graded assignment through the tool, so the assistant can know it has been launched in a graded context directly from the launch itself, the same reliable, system-reported signal Section 3.8 requires, rather than needing to infer it. This makes Canvas a genuine candidate for embedded assessment-mode support, unlike the current Blackboard default of no assistant access inside exam pages at all.

Grade passback is scoped identically to every other adapter: objective items only. Canvas's Assignment and Grade Services can return scores to the gradebook without the student needing to access the tool directly, and the Section 5 grading wall applies to this adapter exactly as it does to every other: automated passback is enabled only for auto-scored objective items, never for applied or subjective work, regardless of how permissive the underlying platform capability is.

> **Note:** Verify before building, the same discipline as every other adapter Confirm the specific licensee's Canvas instance and account-level permissions before assuming self-service install: Developer Key creation and deployment authority can be restricted to certain admin roles or sub-accounts depending on how an institution has configured Canvas, and this should be checked against the specific licensee rather than assumed from the general capability.

## 6.9 The embeddable per-course widget: a fifth adapter

Beyond the LMS-embedded, popout, and standalone-tab deployment patterns already specified, a fifth reach pattern is needed for contexts outside any LTI-capable LMS entirely: a small, embeddable chat widget any website or course page can install directly, in the same spirit as a typical customer-support chat button. This is architecturally a new frontend, not new engine logic; it reaches the same engine behind the same platform-adapter seam as every other deployment surface in this specification.

Each course receives its own unique, generated embed code, an identifier a faculty member or site administrator pastes into a small script snippet on their page, the same pattern established by widely used embeddable chat tools. That identifier is what supplies the course_id the engine already requires for retrieval, per Section 4's existing course-scoped isolation, which does not change to support this adapter, it is simply fed a course identifier through a new channel.

> **Note:** This is a weaker identity guarantee than LTI, and the specification says so plainly An LTI launch, per Section 6.1, supplies a signed, independently verified token the engine can trust cryptographically. A widget embed code is merely declared by whoever installs the script, no independent verification exists that the code is being used by an authorized party or on an authorized page. This is an acceptable tradeoff for a low-stakes, broadly reachable deployment surface, but it must never be treated as equivalent to a verified LTI launch for any purpose requiring real identity assurance, grading, FERPA-covered data handling, or any Tier 3 guardrail decision. Where this adapter is used in a for-credit context, the engine's guardrail tier for that course should default to its most conservative setting rather than assume the same trust level as a verified LTI launch.

Embed code generation, revocation, and per-course management are functions of the operator backend specified in Section 12, not a capability built into the widget itself. A widget's embed code can be revoked centrally without requiring any change to the installing website, the same operational pattern as revoking an API key.

## 6.12 Join-code enrollment: a sixth identity path, distinct from LTI

Beyond the LTI-verified launch, the popout, the standalone tab, and the embeddable widget, a sixth identity path is needed for the Custom TCI LMS and for any deployment context where the assistant is not embedded inside an institutional LMS at all: a student authenticates with a real Google account, then enters a join code, set and distributed by the course's instructor, to gain access to a specific course. This is genuinely simpler to build and simpler for an instructor to use than a roster-matching enrollment model, at the cost of a different, weaker identity guarantee that this section states plainly rather than leaves implicit.

### 6.12.1 What this mechanism actually proves, and what it does not

Google authentication verifies that the email address behind a sign-in is real and controlled by the person signing in. It does not verify that the person is a student officially enrolled in the course whose join code they entered. A join code is a shared secret: whoever has it can join, the same security model as a Wi-Fi password. This is a meaningfully weaker enrollment guarantee than a professor-provided roster would give, and it is an accepted, deliberate tradeoff for the reduction in build complexity and instructor friction it buys, not an oversight.

> **Note:** Why this distinction stops being a footnote the moment grades are involved For tutoring, formative practice, and the memory system specified in Section 3.2, proving this person typed the code the instructor gave out is a perfectly adequate identity guarantee, since the cost of a false enrollment is low, at most an uninvited person receives free tutoring. The stated future plan for this system to become the source of record for grades changes that calculus entirely. A grade must be tied to a verified, accountable identity, not merely someone who possessed a shared code. Before any grading capability is built on top of join-code enrollment, this identity gap must be closed, most plausibly by requiring an institutional email domain match, a secondary verification step, or a graduated trust model where join-code identity is sufficient for tutoring but insufficient on its own for anything that writes to a grade of record, consistent with the grading wall already established in Section 5.

### 6.12.2 Mechanics

Course creation and code generation. An instructor, through the operator backend or faculty dashboard per Sections 11.2 and 12, creates a course and receives a generated join code to distribute to students by whatever means they choose, verbally in class, on a syllabus, or otherwise. No student roster or email list is required in advance.

Student enrollment. A student authenticates with any real Google account of their choosing and enters the join code. On a valid, unexpired code, the student is enrolled in that course, and their authenticated Google identity becomes the durable identifier the interaction history table and every other student-facing record in Section 3 is keyed against, replacing the browser-local-storage placeholder used throughout this build's initial testing.

Instructor-set expiration governs both the code and existing access. An instructor sets an expiration date for a course, typically aligned to a term's end. On that date, two things happen together: the join code stops accepting new enrollments, and existing enrolled students lose active access to that course's tutoring, memory, and history. This mirrors how access to a real course naturally ends when the term does, and treats an indefinitely lingering tutor as the wrong default rather than a convenience.

Expired data is preserved, not deleted, following the same reasoning already established for audit logs. A student's accumulated interaction history for an expired course becomes inactive and unreachable through ordinary tutoring use, but it is not deleted, since Section 3.2's own retention rule already established the same distinction for a different reason, a grade dispute, an academic-integrity appeal, or an accreditation review can arise after a course formally ends, and this system's own stated future role as a source of grades makes that possibility concrete rather than hypothetical. Deletion on expiration would destroy exactly the evidence that role would eventually require to exist.

### 6.12.3 This is the tutoring half of the system, and the boundary with future professor-facing tools is deliberate

Everything specified in this section, sign-in, course assignment, session memory, and accumulated history, is the student-facing identity and memory layer, squarely Section 3's territory. The professor-facing capabilities this system is stated to require in the future, grading, rosters, and other instructor tools, belong with Section 11.2's faculty dashboard and Section 12's operator backend, not folded into this identity mechanism. Keeping this boundary explicit now, before either side is built out further, is the same discipline that kept the LMS and the assistant as separate documents from the beginning of this project: a system that tries to be both a student's tutor and an instructor's gradebook from one undifferentiated design serves neither role well.

### 6.12.4 A correction worth stating precisely: this stores real student data

A Google email address is personal, identifying information, and associating it with a course and a history of interactions is storing student data in exactly the sense FERPA and this specification's own privacy sections address, not an exception from those obligations. The accurate, narrower claim is that this mechanism does not store grades, submissions, or academic records, only identity and interaction history, which is a real and meaningful scope boundary, but it is a boundary on what kind of student data is stored, not an absence of student data. Treating this system as exempt from the access-control, retention, and deletion obligations already established elsewhere in this specification because it only holds login and history information would be the same category of mistake as the answer_bearing tag existing without being enforced: a control that looks satisfied on the surface while doing nothing underneath.

### 6.12.5 Future identity tier: school-issued email, the concrete answer to the grading-readiness question

Section 6.12.1 named the precondition explicitly: join-code identity, on its own, proves possession of a shared code, not verified enrollment, and this is insufficient for anything that writes to a grade of record. This subsection names the concrete future mechanism that closes that gap, rather than leaving it as an abstract future decision. A future version of this specification requires a student to authenticate using their school-issued email address, not any Google account of their choosing, and for that authentication to be genuinely tied to the institution's own control over that identity, most plausibly through Google Workspace for Education sign-in restricted to the institution's own domain, or a direct integration with the school's internal identity system, single sign-on or an equivalent institutional authentication standard, rather than TCI independently verifying an arbitrary email address.

This is a materially stronger identity guarantee than join-code enrollment as specified in this version, because a school-issued email is an identity the institution itself controls, can verify against its own enrollment records, and can revoke, in exactly the way a personal Google account cannot. It is also a materially larger undertaking: it requires an institution to be willing to integrate TCI's system with its own identity infrastructure, the same category of institutional trust and technical integration already discussed for LTI in Section 6.4, and it should be sequenced the same way, pursued when a real institutional relationship and a real grading requirement make it concrete, not built speculatively ahead of that need.

> **Note:** The relationship between this and Section 6.12's current, simpler version Join-code enrollment with any Google account remains the correct, sufficient mechanism for tutoring and for the Custom TCI LMS's early, non-grading use, and it should not be over-built into an institutional identity integration before there is a real institution and a real grading requirement asking for one. This subsection exists so that when that moment arrives, the answer is already specified rather than improvised, and so no grading capability is ever built on top of join-code identity alone in the meantime.

## 6.13 A real credential exposure incident, and what it changed

During local development, a diagnostic command run to inspect the structure of the environment file holding this project's secrets instead printed those secrets in plaintext into a tool-output transcript, exposing the Anthropic API key, the Voyage AI key, the Supabase service credential, and a temporary site password in use at the time. A second, related exposure occurred shortly after: a newly generated replacement credential was pasted in a label-colon-value format rather than the expected key-equals-value format, which defeated a redaction pattern that only masked content following an equals sign, exposing that credential as well before it was ever put into use. All affected credentials were treated as compromised and rotated, regardless of how briefly or narrowly they were exposed, consistent with the principle that a secret which has been displayed anywhere outside its intended, controlled locations no longer carries the guarantee that makes it a secret, independent of whether anyone else is known to have seen it.

A separate, compounding problem surfaced during the same incident: the environment file was being silently and repeatedly overwritten mid-session by a stale text editor window that had the file open from earlier in the session and rewrote it with an outdated in-memory copy on every save, regardless of what was actually being edited at the time. This erased unrelated, correct edits made in between, and was the direct cause of the second credential's exposure, since the file's format had already been corrupted by the time a value was added to it.

> **Note:** Standing rules adopted from this incident Inspecting the structure of a file containing secrets must use name-only extraction or line-length checks, never a command that can print raw content, since a redaction pattern tuned for one expected format will not necessarily catch a value in an unexpected one. A file holding live secrets should never be held open in more than one editor or process at a time during an active session, since a stale window silently reasserting old content is indistinguishable, from the outside, from a successful edit, until something inexplicably reverts. Where a legacy credential format cannot be individually rotated or revoked, confirmed directly against the vendor's own current documentation before assuming otherwise, the correct response is migration to a newer, independently revocable credential format, not repeated attempts to rotate the unrotatable one.

Two small, low-risk cleanup items remain from this incident, worth naming so they are not silently forgotten rather than because either poses an active risk: the legacy Supabase service-role credential is no longer referenced anywhere in the application's code following the migration to the newer secret key format, but the now-unused environment variable itself has not yet been removed from either the local environment file or the hosting platform's configuration. Separately, the current hosting framework has deprecated the conventional middleware filename in favor of a new one; the existing file continues to function under its old name with only a harmless warning, and renaming it is routine technical debt, not a functional concern.

> **Note:** This work is deployed and independently verified on the live production domain, not just committed Every fix described in this section, the rotated credentials, the temporary password gate, the Supabase secret key migration, and the corrected Anthropic authentication, has been deployed to production through the deliberate manual deploy step this specification requires, and verified directly against the live domain rather than assumed from local testing: unauthenticated and incorrect-password requests are blocked on both the visible site and a direct request to the chat endpoint with a fully valid payload, while the correct password grants access and a real request returns a complete, correctly grounded response, confirming the rotated credentials and the workspace-scoped Anthropic authentication work together on the actual production system a real visitor would reach. The entire production site, including the sign-in flow itself, currently sits behind this temporary password gate as a deliberate consequence of the exposure described above; this is expected and should not be mistaken for the site being broken. Removing the gate later is a matter of clearing the password value from the hosting platform's configuration, since the gate is written to take no action at all when that value is unset.

# 7. What Standalone Actually Costs

The demo was a single HTML file with the API key baked into client-side code, which is fine for a demo and catastrophic for production, because a client-side key can be extracted and used to run up the account. Going standalone fixes that by keeping the key server-side, but it converts the project from a prompt-engineering exercise into a small hosted software product. The spec owns that honestly. The following obligations are real, ongoing, and were invisible in the demo.

| Obligation | Why it exists | Nature of the cost |
|---|---|---|
| A hosted backend running continuously | Receives student messages, injects context, calls the model server-side, returns answers. Key never client-side. | Monthly hosting cost and an uptime responsibility. |
| Identity via LTI, per tenant | For-credit logging and personalization require verified student identity from the LMS. | One-time integration build per licensee school, then maintenance. |
| The knowledge store | Hundreds of versioned course sources the engine retrieves from and pins to. | Storage, an authoring-to-source pipeline, and update discipline. |
| Audit logging at FERPA grade | You must be able to reconstruct what the assistant told any student. | Storage, access control, retention policy, legal weight. |
| Model API usage | Every answer is a metered model call. | Per-interaction cost that scales with student volume. Budget and rate-limit. |

None of this argues against the standalone decision, which is correct. It argues for pricing and staffing the assistant as the small SaaS product it is. The demo trained an intuition that the hard part is the persona and the prompt. The hard part is identity, groundedness, audit, and keeping a hosted service alive. Any plan that budgets only for the prompt will underdeliver on exactly the parts that make the assistant trustworthy.

> **Note:** This obligations table predates the RAG architecture in Section 4 and undercounts its cost The original version of this section was written before multi-source, embeddings-based retrieval was specified. Two real, ongoing costs from Section 4 are not reflected in the table above and belong in the same budgeting conversation: embedding compute, generating and storing vector representations for every chunk across every course and every attached supplementary source, and re-embedding cost, triggered every time a course's content is edited or a new textbook is attached, since Section 4.5's versioning discipline means a content change is not just a database update but a re-embedding event. At the scale of hundreds of courses across multiple licensees, this is not a one-time setup cost; it recurs every time content changes, and it should be sized and budgeted alongside the per-interaction model cost already named above, not treated as a rounding error beneath it.

# 9. Open Questions to Resolve Before Build

These are the decisions this spec deliberately leaves open because they need your input or external verification, not because they can be deferred. They are grouped by severity rather than left as one flat list. Items added in v2.7 reflect gaps surfaced by real implementation, not speculative future work.

## 9.1 Safety-critical: resolve before any real student uses the assistant

Distress-signal detection mechanism, specified in v3.6. Section 3.6 requires crisis and wellbeing concerns to route to a human immediately; this section now specifies how detection actually works, rather than leaving it as an unresolved priority.

Detection is a dedicated classifier call, separate from the tutoring model, run concurrently with retrieval on every exchange but able to replace the response entirely, unlike the after-the-fact comprehension classifier, which cannot affect the answer already given. It must function for anonymous, unauthenticated students, so it cannot depend on student_id. Classification is graded across five levels, not binary, because a binary flag either fires on ordinary exam stress or is tuned so high it misses real signal: none, no signal, nothing changes; academic_frustration, ordinary struggle with material, handled entirely by the existing tutoring pattern's acknowledgment step, no resources surfaced, nothing logged, since surfacing crisis-adjacent resources to a frustrated student insults them and erodes trust in the assistant generally; personal_distress, a real wellbeing signal with no indication of danger, answered with a model-generated reflection of the specific disclosure under hard constraints, no diagnosis, no coping advice, no tutoring task that turn, followed by fixed text naming the assistant's real limits and handing control back to the student; possible_risk, genuinely ambiguous language that could indicate danger, answered with fixed text built around a direct, non-euphemistic question rather than a softened one, since evidence-based crisis-response guidance is explicit that asking directly does not increase risk and may reduce it; and crisis, explicit or strongly implied risk of harm, answered with fixed, sourced text and no model generation at all, the same starve-rather-than-discipline principle Section 3.8 uses for assessment mode, since fixed text cannot drift, cannot be argued out of, and cannot be prompt-injected.

The tie-break rule at the two adjacent boundaries runs in opposite directions, and this must be stated as two explicit rules, not one general caution instruction, since the costly error is different at each boundary. Between academic_frustration and personal_distress, the costly error is over-firing, so genuine ambiguity resolves toward the lower level. Between possible_risk and crisis, the costly error is under-firing, so genuine ambiguity resolves toward the higher level, including when a student refers to something serious indirectly, a pronoun or a euphemism standing in for a stated referent already present in their own words or the immediate conversation, which must be treated as the referent being present, not absent.

A student's later retraction or claim that they were joking does not downgrade a crisis classification, since minimization immediately following a disclosure is itself a recognized pattern, not credible evidence the original disclosure was false. The classification stands; only the response's presentation changes, a first crisis response in a conversation delivers the full fixed text, and a repeat crisis classification in the same conversation delivers a brief, sourced acknowledgment rather than the full text again, since repeating the complete script at a student who has already received it once reads as being talked at rather than heard.

A second, independent binary signal, interpersonal_harm, runs alongside the five-level classification on every exchange and is not itself a response level. It indicates whether the disclosed content is shaped like harassment, assault, stalking, or dating or domestic violence, distinct from general wellbeing distress. When true, it bypasses the standard multi-occurrence pattern threshold that otherwise governs when a human is notified of accumulating lower-level events, since a disclosure in this category warrants notification-worthiness on its first occurrence, not its third, independent of any Title IX-specific handling, per 9.4's Title IX note below.

Fixed response text is sourced from published, evidence-based crisis-response guidance, not freehand-authored, on the reasoning that neither an engineering team nor an operator is qualified to write crisis-response language from a list of required content alone. Crisis-adjacent levels, possible_risk and crisis, draw their structure and required content from 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline's own guidance and the BeThe1To framework's five action steps, Ask, Be There, Keep Them Safe, Help Them Connect, Follow Up, mapped honestly against what a stateless course assistant can and cannot actually do; it cannot restrict means, cannot promise to follow up, and must never promise confidentiality, since the exchange is logged. The personal_distress level, carrying no indication of danger, is sourced instead from SAMHSA's non-crisis guidance on how to talk to someone about getting help, since applying a suicide-prevention framework's crisis steps to a level explicitly defined as carrying no danger signal would be the wrong source for the case.

A model-generation gap surfaced during implementation and is recorded here as a durable finding, not a passing note: explicit prompt instructions against stock openers and generic sympathy phrases, present in two separate places in the response prompt, both failed to prevent the model from converging on a small set of templated openers across repeated real samples. The reliable fix was structural, a hard length bound on the generated portion, not an additional instruction. This is treated as a general finding about this runtime model under this kind of constraint, not specific to this one response: where a response combines genuine per-case variation with fixed required content, the variable portion should be kept as small and structurally bounded as possible, and the fixed portion should be sourced text, not an instruction asking the model to reliably sound spontaneous while saying the same required things every time.

Events at personal_distress and above are stored in a dedicated table, not folded into the general interaction-history table, since that table's schema assumes a non-null student_id and a comprehension-tutoring shape neither of which fits an anonymous or crisis exchange. Retention is two-tier: the raw disclosed message text is purged automatically after thirty days, exceeding the pattern-detection window and a reasonable follow-up period, while event metadata, level, timestamp, course, and whether the message was purged, is retained for one hundred eighty days to preserve pattern-detection and defensibility value without retaining a distressed student's actual words indefinitely. Access to this table is restricted at the database layer to a named human who has affirmatively committed to a review cadence, per the single-point-of-failure risk named in the risk register; there is no dashboard or alerting surface as of this version, so a logged event is visible only when that named reviewer actually looks, and a course must not be opened to student access until that reviewer and cadence are on file, enforced structurally rather than left as a policy to remember.

A durability principle applies to this entire mechanism and is stated here because it was violated once during implementation and caught only by an unrelated test: a classification failure, the classifier call itself failing rather than returning a low level, must fail loudly and record that failure durably, and must never silently resolve to the same value as no signal detected, since those two outcomes are otherwise indistinguishable to everything downstream. See the new standing principle in Section 13.5.

Struggling-student trigger thresholds: the specific numbers used in this draft, two failed gate quiz attempts, two explanation-adaptation cycles, are reasonable starting points, not validated ones. Confirm or adjust once real student interaction data exists, since an unvalidated threshold either misses students who need help or over-triggers in a way that erodes trust in the assistant.

Answer-bearing chunk tagging accuracy: Section 3.8's retrieval-starvation defense is only as reliable as the human tagging behind it, per Section 4.1. An untagged or mistagged answer-bearing chunk defeats the primary defense silently. This needs an actual review step in the course-build workflow, not a one-time instruction to tag carefully.

Real, verified course-identity resolution: course_id and student_id are currently hardcoded placeholders in the live chat UI, not values derived from a verified LTI launch or any other real identity source. Every piece of Section 3's memory and adaptive retrieval work, while individually tested and correct, currently operates against a single fixed course. This is the highest-priority remaining gap before any second course or any real student population can safely use the system.

## 9.4 Product and positioning

Persona identity per program, resolved in v3.6: Nancy is retired fully, and no surface carries a named persona. Each program has an unnamed, role-based voice instead, ACI's warm practitioner register, AIE's graduate-peer register, and TCI University Online's collegial-academic register, stored per-course via a program column that governs tone only and is read by nothing else, no guardrail, capability, refusal, academic-integrity, privacy, or access-control logic. The reasoning: a named persona invites a level of relational trust and continuity a stateless, session-bound system cannot responsibly sustain, particularly given that distress-signal detection, Section 9.1, is precisely the case where a student's sense of who they are talking to matters most and where overclaiming continuity is most costly. Naming may be revisited once distress detection has real usage history behind it, not before.

White-label surfacing: how much of the assistant is co-branded to the licensee, given that TCI retains all IP and the licensee white-labels delivery.

Runtime model decision, recorded: the assistant runs on Claude Sonnet via the Anthropic API. This is a cost-appropriate choice for retrieval-grounded tutoring, and it is precisely why assessment-mode enforcement is structural (retrieval starvation, Section 3.8) rather than dependent on model-level instruction-holding. If the runtime model ever changes, the structural enforcement stays; it is not a workaround to be removed when a stronger model arrives.

The Custom TCI LMS, the gated front-end pattern from Section 6.6, and its own build plan and pre-launch security review gate, are specified in a separate companion document. This document remains the assistant engine specification: it governs the model-facing service, its groundedness, escalation, grading boundary, pedagogy, and LMS portability, and it does not own student authentication or a gradebook on any platform, including a future TCI-built one.

Operator backend scope and access, per new Section 12: confirm who has access beyond the founder at first, and how course and client provisioning should work, before any second real client exists.

Real authentication for the operator backend and faculty dashboard, per Section 12.3a: neither system has a working login yet. Confirm the operator authentication approach first, since it is the smaller, more immediately needed system, and treat faculty and institutional authentication as a separate decision, potentially involving institutional identity integration, once a real licensee makes the requirements concrete.

SOC 2 readiness gaps, per the new Section 6.10 table: the remaining gaps are predominantly process and documentation, an access-control policy, a change-management process, a privacy policy reviewed by counsel, rather than missing technical controls. These should be prioritized ahead of any real institutional deal that would require a SOC 2 report, given the multi-month audit window Section 6.4 already establishes, but not built speculatively before a concrete deal makes the timeline real.

Cyber liability insurance and a legally reviewed incident response plan, per the new Section 6.11 diligence checklist: these are the two buyer-diligence items most likely to actually block or slow a real sale or licensing conversation if left unaddressed, and unlike most of this specification's remaining gaps, neither is engineering work. Sequence both as real business and legal tasks ahead of any serious institutional conversation, not as items waiting on further code.

Join-code enrollment's path to grading readiness: Section 6.12.5 now names the concrete future mechanism, school-issued email authentication tied to the institution's own identity control, either via domain-restricted Google Workspace for Education sign-in or a direct institutional identity integration. The open question is timing and sequencing, pursuing this only once a real institutional relationship and a real grading requirement make it concrete, not building it speculatively, and ensuring no grading capability is built on join-code identity alone before it exists.

Per-course access mode and escalation toggle implementation, per Section 12.2a: confirm the default state for each new course type as the operator backend is actually built, a for-credit course with a known professor of record should default to escalation enabled, while a certificate or public-access course should default to disabled until an operator deliberately opts in, and this default logic should be tested explicitly rather than left to whatever a form happens to initialize to.

Chat-route identity and enrollment verification: decide deliberately whether the chat endpoint should independently verify the student's identity against their session and confirm their enrollment in the course being discussed, the same discipline already applied to the enrollment endpoint, rather than leaving this an open, undecided hardening gap.

Pre-authentication interaction history retention: decide whether interaction-history rows written before real authentication existed, now orphaned from any verified identity, should be purged as test noise or retained as a historical record, rather than left in an undecided state indefinitely.

Legacy credential cleanup: the superseded Supabase service-role environment variable is no longer referenced anywhere in the codebase following the migration described in Section 6.13, but remains present in both the local environment and the hosting platform's configuration. Low risk, but worth removing once confirmed nothing else depends on it, rather than left as permanent, unexplained clutter.

SaaS-readiness posture: architecture should remain compatible with future multi-tenant resale without building multi-tenant infrastructure, billing, or self-serve admin now, ahead of a first paying client. This is a deliberate scope boundary, not an oversight, and should be revisited only when a real near-term client makes it worth building.

Title IX and interpersonal-harm disclosures, a deliberate scope boundary recorded in v3.6, not an oversight. A dedicated detection layer, an automated email-and-transcript notification system, and a syllabus-sourced institutional coordinator lookup were all considered and deliberately not built, for two reasons that hold independently of engineering effort. First, responsible-employee status and the correct reporting pathway are defined by each institution's own policy, not by this specification, and there is no established basis for treating an AI system itself as capable of holding or discharging that status; the safe design posture is to route a qualifying disclosure to a human already unambiguously covered by the institution's own policy, never to have the assistant attempt to act as that role itself. Second, unlike crisis-response guidance, there is no universal, always-correct resource equivalent to a national crisis line for this category, since the correct next step depends entirely on the specific institution's actual Title IX process and current coordinator contact, neither of which this specification can supply or verify on an institution's behalf. What was built instead, per the interpersonal_harm risk-register entry, is narrow: an independent classification signal that recognizes this category of disclosure and bypasses the standard notification-pattern threshold, so a human who already holds a real reporting obligation learns of it on first occurrence rather than waiting behind a threshold tuned for ordinary wellbeing noise. The fuller apparatus remains blocked on two concrete inputs from real institutional relationships: verified, current Title IX coordinator contact information obtained the same way any crisis resource is obtained, never invented or inferred, and explicit confirmation of who at a given institution holds responsible-employee status under that institution's own policy.

# 10. Interface, Continued: The Course Dashboard

Sections 3 and 4 specified the interface for the assistant's specific behaviors as each behavior was defined. One interface surface is not tied to a single behavior: the overall course dashboard a student sees when they are not actively in a conversation with the assistant, the home view of a course inside the LMS. This section specifies that surface and the design principles that hold the whole experience together.

## 10.1 The dashboard's job

The dashboard is where a student lands, sees where they are in a course, and decides what to do next. It sits alongside, not inside, the LMS content-delivery surface already specified in the LMS Build Plan's Phase 2; this section defines what the dashboard shows about the assistant's role in the course, not the module and lesson navigation itself, which belongs to the LMS.

Current position, plainly stated. Which module the student is in, and, per the optional progress reflection in Section 3.2, an entry point to see their own study-aid summary if they choose to.

A visible, inviting entry point to the assistant, not a buried icon. Given the assistant is meant to feel like a genuine teaching presence in the course, its entry point deserves real visual weight on the dashboard, not a small chat bubble in a corner easily overlooked.

Any pending proactive check-in or escalation notice, per Section 3.3, surfaced honestly. If the assistant has something to offer the student, it is visible here as well as in the chat itself, so a student who has not opened the chat recently still sees that something is waiting for them.

## 10.2 Design principles across the whole experience

A few principles recur across every interface decision made in this document, and stating them once, together, makes it possible to judge a future interface addition against a consistent standard rather than reinventing the reasoning each time.

Calm over clever. Every surface, the chat, the check-in, the assessment-mode indicator, the dashboard, favors clarity and a settled visual tone over novelty. A tool a student relies on throughout a course should feel steady, not attention-seeking.

Honesty is visible, not just backend policy. Provenance citations, the non-verification disclaimer on supplementary media, the assessment-mode indicator, and the escalation notice are all specified as things the student actually sees, not just internal rules the engine follows silently. A compliance rule that is invisible to the student is weaker than one the student can see operating.

Warmth without surveillance. The interface reflects the assistant's teacher-like register (Section 3.1) without ever presenting the student's own data back to them as a tracked scorecard (Section 3.2), and never over-notifies in the name of care (Section 3.3).

Every new behavior gets an interface before it ships, not after. This document was deliberately built by defining each behavior and its interface together, rather than specifying all behavior first and designing screens afterward, because a screen designed against settled behavior fits it precisely, and a screen designed against assumed behavior often needs to be rebuilt once the real behavior is defined. Future additions to the assistant should follow the same discipline.

Specifying visual polish before the underlying layout has been used and tested would risk the same mistake this whole document has worked to avoid elsewhere: designing a surface before the thing it serves is settled.

## 11.4 Visual design system for the student surface, specified in v3.7

This section performs the visual pass Section 11.3 deliberately deferred, now that the underlying wireframes have been validated against real use. It specifies a token system, colors, typography, layout principles, and states the reasoning behind each choice, since a palette handed over without the reasoning behind it degrades into arbitrary substitution the first time someone wants to change it. It does not yet exist as production code; it is a specification a build session should implement against, the same relationship every other wireframe in this document has to its eventual build.

Persona is deliberately cosmetic per Section 9.4, one unnamed voice per program, and the visual system follows the identical discipline for the same reason. There is one visual system, not three, applied consistently across ACI, AIE, and TCI University Online, with only the smallest possible accent-color variation permitted between programs, never a full re-skin. Fragmenting the visual language by program would recreate, visually, exactly the risk that keeping persona unnamed and role-based was meant to avoid: a student forming a different level of trust or a different expectation of the assistant's authority based on which program's colors happen to be on screen, rather than on anything the assistant actually is or does.

Color. Five named values form the base palette. Paper, `#F6F4EF`, a warm, slightly cool-shifted off-white used for the primary background, chosen specifically to read as a page or a document rather than an app chrome, and deliberately not the near-cream tone that has become a generic marker of AI-generated interfaces. Ink, `#20262B`, a deep charcoal-navy used for primary text, chosen over pure black because a working reading surface a student may sit with for a long tutoring session should be slightly softer than maximum contrast. Rule, `#D6D0C4`, a warm taupe used only for hairline borders and dividers, never as a fill. Accent, `#8A6D3B`, a muted, brass-toned ochre used sparingly and only for the assistant's own active turn indicator, links, and primary buttons, chosen deliberately outside the terracotta-and-warm-clay family that has become a recognizable tell of a different AI product's own interaction color. Signal, `#3D5A73`, a muted slate-blue used exclusively for system-state changes, the assessment-mode banner and any future banner of its kind, and never used for anything conversational, so that a student learns through repeated exposure that blue-grey means the system itself has changed state, while the ochre accent means the assistant is simply present and responding.

The wellbeing and crisis states specified in Section 9.1 receive their own explicit, named treatment, since getting this wrong has a real cost a generic error-state color would not. Quiet, `#EDEAE2`, a deeper, calmer neutral than the paper background, is the background shift used when a personal_distress, possible_risk, or crisis response is on screen. This is a deliberate departure from the visual convention an alert or error state would normally use: no red, no orange, no saturated warning color of any kind, and no pulsing, flashing, or other attention-seeking motion. The design principle is that the interface goes quiet rather than loud at exactly the moment a student is in genuine distress, since a visually alarming treatment risks amplifying the moment rather than steadying it, and a student already in crisis does not need the screen itself signaling emergency back at them. The assessment-mode banner's signal blue and the wellbeing state's quiet background must never be visually confused with each other; they represent categorically different kinds of state change, one procedural and one personal, and sharing a visual language between them would blur a distinction Section 9.1 and Section 3.8 both depend on students perceiving correctly.

Typography. Two typefaces, clearly distinct from each other, each doing one job. A humanist serif, in the register of Source Serif 4 or Lora, is used only for authored course content and module titles, the material that originates from the course itself, so that a student learns to associate the serif with the textbook and syllabus voice. A humanist sans, in the register of Inter or IBM Plex Sans, is used for everything else, all interface chrome, all system messages, all of the assistant's own conversational text, since the chat surface is read at length and in small sizes, and a working tool's primary reading surface should prioritize sustained legibility over character. This pairing is a structural choice, not a decorative one: the two typefaces encode the same distinction Section 3.5's provenance marker already makes explicit, this came from the course, this is the assistant talking, so a careful reader can tell the two apart even before reading the provenance marker itself.

Layout. The chat surface is a single centered column, not a wide, edge-to-edge layout, with a comfortable reading measure rather than a maximized one. Assistant responses are left-aligned text blocks with generous line height, not rounded speech-bubble shapes with tails, a deliberate departure from consumer chat-app visual language, since the audience is an adult, often graduate-level student, and a cartoonish messaging aesthetic undersells the register Section 2's persona layer already establishes for AIE and TCI University Online specifically. The provenance marker and flag affordance from Section 11.1 sit quietly beneath a response in the sans typeface at a reduced size, present and legible but never competing with the response itself for attention.

Motion is used once, deliberately, and otherwise avoided. The single sanctioned moment is the assistant's response appearing, a brief, single fade, never a per-character typing animation that would slow down a student trying to read quickly, and never any hover or entrance animation on ordinary interface elements, consistent with the frontend-design principle that scattered, ambient motion reads as generic rather than considered. No motion of any kind accompanies the shift into a wellbeing or crisis state; the quiet background change happens instantly, since motion drawing attention to that exact transition would contradict the same reasoning that ruled out alarm coloring for it.

> **Note:** What this section does not yet resolve. This is a token system and a set of stated principles, not a built, tested interface. It has not been checked against accessibility contrast ratios in its exact stated hex values, has not been tested on a small mobile viewport, and has not been reviewed against a real screen reader pass. These are implementation-time verification steps, not open design questions, and should be treated as required gates before this system ships, the same discipline this document already applies to every other component that looks complete on paper.

## 11.5 Institution administration: a tier between the section and the operator, first specified in v3.8

Section 11.2 specifies a dashboard scoped to one section, for the people who hold a role on it, professor, escalation recipient, wellbeing reader, general staff. Section 12 specifies the operator backend, scoped to TCI itself, across every course and every client. Between those two sits a real tier this document had not previously named: a role scoped to one institution, spanning every course that institution licenses, not one section and not the whole business.

The evidence that this tier is real, not speculative, is TCI's own confirmed business shape, not a hypothetical future case. La Sierra currently licenses both the TCI Undergraduate Business Catalog and the TCI MBA Catalog simultaneously, with Burman and PUC queued next for the same multi-course relationship. Any institution licensing more than one course needs someone on their side able to see status across all of them, which no role specified before this version could represent: a professor's view is scoped to a section, and nothing below the operator's own account spans multiple courses under one licensee.

This closes a real structural gap the section-scoped role model could not have closed by extension. `section_staff`, however it eventually grows, is scoped to one section by its own foreign key; there is no way to widen a section-scoped role into an institution-spanning one without changing what the role's key actually references. The correct fix is a distinct entity at the correct level, not a broader permission on an existing one.

**What this requires, structurally, that does not exist anywhere in the current schema.** `courses` today has no owner: no `institution_id`, no `institutions` table, nothing recording which licensee a given course belongs to. Introducing this tier means adding an `institutions` table and a required `courses.institution_id` foreign key, which is a genuine change to an existing table, not purely additive at the schema level the way most of this build has been, and it needs a backfill: MKTG365 needs an institution row created and attached before the constraint can be required rather than optional. Institution-level staff should follow the same accepted-pending, actor-audited pattern already proven for `section_staff`, in its own table scoped to `institution_id` rather than `section_id`, not a new pattern invented for a fourth role.

**Capability model, resolved.** Institution administration carries one unconditional capability regardless of any course-level grant: creating new professor accounts within its own institution. Beyond that, institution admin's access to any given course defaults to read-only, structurally, not by convention. Editing a specific course, changing its configuration, its staff, anything a professor can do today, requires that course's own professor to affirmatively grant institution admin that access for that specific course; there is no institution-wide setting that opens every course under a licensee at once, and the absence of a grant means the course stays closed to editing regardless of anything else.

This is the same underlying principle already proven for `section_staff`'s escalation recipient and wellbeing reader roles, run in the reverse direction. There, a role only becomes live once the person being assigned to it affirmatively accepts. Here, a professor affirmatively grants access to their own institution admin, rather than accepting a role offered to them, but the governing idea is identical: authority over something this consequential is never live merely because someone with a higher position in the hierarchy wants it, it requires an actual, affirmative act by the person on the other side of that authority.

TCI-level access is unconditional in both directions: it can edit everything, with no grant required from anyone, the correct shape for the top of this hierarchy.

**What this version still does not resolve.** Whether TCI wants an actual built interface for its own top-level, all-clients view now that institutions exist as a real entity, or whether that continues to be served by direct database access the way it has been throughout this build, remains undecided. This should be resolved before any institution-admin migration is written, the same discipline already applied to every other structural decision in this document: a real, stated answer, not a default assumed by proceeding.

# 12. The Operator Backend: Managing Courses and Clients, Not Just One Course

Section 11.2 specified a faculty dashboard for a single instructor managing a single course, content sources, struggle patterns, escalations, feedback signature, audit access. That remains correct and necessary. It is not sufficient for TCI's own operational need: a console for TCI, as the operator, to manage courses and clients across the whole catalog, not one instructor's one course. This is a distinct system with its own real requirements, specified here rather than folded silently into the faculty dashboard, since conflating the two would produce a tool that serves neither role well.

## 12.1 Why this is a different system, not a bigger version of the faculty dashboard

The faculty dashboard answers a scoped question: what does this instructor need to see about their own course. The operator backend answers a structurally different question: what does TCI need to see and do across every course and, eventually, every licensee client. A faculty member should never see another instructor's course; an operator, by the nature of the role, legitimately needs visibility across all of them. Building one interface to serve both roles would either over-expose data to faculty or under-serve the operator's actual job, provisioning new courses, managing embed codes, and eventually onboarding new clients.

> **Note:** A third tier, between these two, is specified separately in Section 11.5: institution administration, scoped to one licensee across every course that licensee licenses, neither one section nor the whole business. This section's operator backend remains the top tier, TCI's own view across everything; it is not being expanded to also mean institution-level oversight, which has its own distinct scope and its own open questions.

## 12.2 Core functions

Course provisioning without hand-written scripts. Today, ingesting a new course requires a human operator working directly with an engineering session, running the ingestion pipeline by hand. The operator backend wraps the existing, already-generic ingestion pipeline described in Section 4 in a real interface: upload a course's content, review a parsed preview before anything is committed, consistent with the same preview-before-publish discipline already established for the LMS's own zip importer, and publish. The pipeline logic does not change; what changes is that a human no longer needs to be an engineer to run it.

Catalog-wide visibility. A single view listing every ingested course, its knowledge source completeness per Section 4.4's content-gap tooling, its license status, and its answer_bearing tagging coverage, so an operator can see at a glance which courses are fully hardened and which still carry open compliance work, rather than needing to check each course individually.

Embed code management for the widget adapter. Generating, viewing, and revoking the per-course embed codes specified in Section 6.9 is a function of this backend, not the widget itself. Revocation must take effect immediately and require no change to the installing website, the same operational pattern as revoking an API key.

Client and tenant management, scoped to what is actually needed today, not built ahead of need. At minimum, an operator needs to associate courses with a client or institution for reporting and eventual billing purposes. Full multi-tenant infrastructure, self-serve client onboarding, per-client billing, client-facing admin accounts, is explicitly out of scope until a real client requires it, consistent with the standing principle against building ahead of validated demand. The data model should not preclude this future state, course and client identifiers should already be separable, since retrofitting tenant isolation into a system that never distinguished clients would be far more disruptive than designing the identifiers correctly from the start.

## 12.2a Per-course access mode and escalation configuration

Every course carries two independent operator-configurable settings, exposed together in the same course-configuration view, since both govern how a course is reached and what obligations attach to using it, and an operator setting one up naturally needs to decide both at once.

Access mode. An operator toggles, per course, how that course is reached: fully public, reachable by anyone who has the URL with no enrollment step at all, useful for rapid internal testing and early demonstrations; join-code enrollment per Section 6.12, requiring a student to authenticate and enter a course-specific code; or, once available, institutional login per Section 6.12.5, requiring a verified school-issued identity. This setting exists specifically so the operator, testing pieces of the system independently, is never forced to build out full enrollment machinery just to try something, while a real course serving real students can be configured with the access rigor it actually needs.

Escalation availability. Escalation, per Section 3.6, is only meaningful when a real, accountable human exists to receive it. A course's escalation setting is therefore not a function of whether an individual student is anonymous or logged in, both can coexist on the same course, but of whether the course itself has a designated responsible party. A for-credit course with a professor of record has escalation enabled by default, since that responsible party already exists structurally. A certificate or non-credit program has no such party by default, and the operator setting it up sees an explicit toggle to enable escalation only if a real person has agreed to receive it. Escalation must never be silently enabled for a course with no designated recipient, since an escalation with nowhere real to go is worse than no escalation at all, it creates the appearance of a safety net that does not exist.

> **Note:** Correction, v3.6. This section originally described enabled and recipient as two independently set values, an operator toggles escalation on and separately supplies a recipient. That design is wrong and was found wrong by a real instance of it: a live for-credit course had escalation enabled with no recipient on file, in direct contradiction of its own intended default, because nothing prevented the two values from disagreeing. The corrected design does not have two independently settable values at all. Whether escalation is enabled is derived automatically from whether a verified recipient exists, present means enabled, absent means disabled, so the two cannot disagree by construction rather than by discipline. This is stated here as the actual design, not as a caveat beside the original description, since a schema that merely constrained enabled-without-a-recipient, one direction only, is exactly the asymmetry that let a recipient sit on file with escalation still reading as disabled, the state the same live course was actually found in.

> **Note:** Why these two settings are configured together rather than separately An operator standing up a new course for internal testing wants public access and no escalation obligation, since there is no real student population and no responsible party yet. A professor launching a real for-credit course wants join-code or institutional access and escalation enabled by default, since a real professor of record already exists. A certificate program operator wants join-code access with escalation as a deliberate, explicit choice, since the responsible party for a certificate program is not automatic the way a professor of record is. Presenting both settings in one place makes the actual, coupled decision, who can reach this course, and is anyone accountable if a student needs help, visible as one decision rather than two disconnected toggles an operator might set inconsistently.

## 12.2b Professor self-service content contribution: a second ingestion path, and the trust boundary it requires

A second path into a course's knowledge collection is specified here, distinct from the operator path in Section 12.2: a professor of record contributing or updating their own course's content directly, without an operator or an engineering session as an intermediary. This is specified, not built, and it must not be built until the trust boundary below is implemented as a structural gate, not a documented intention, since the failure mode it guards against is the same one the original answer_bearing leak demonstrated, at a larger scale and with less oversight per instance.

The reasoning that makes this a genuinely different problem from operator ingestion, not a smaller version of it. MKTG365's content was ingested by an operator who reviewed it personally, and closing a single real leak in that content still required five independent adversarial test runs before the fix could be trusted. A professor uploading their own material has no equivalent review built into the act of uploading, and a professor is, correctly, not expected to understand this system's own retrieval-safety mechanisms, answer_bearing and assessment_scope, well enough to self-certify that their upload has been tagged correctly. Treating professor-uploaded content as safe to retrieve immediately upon upload would mean the single most carefully hardened defense in this entire specification has an uncontrolled second door into it.

The gate, stated as the non-negotiable design requirement this path is built around: no content contributed through this path is retrievable by any student, under any access mode, until it has passed through the same tagging and licensing discipline already required of the operator path, and that passage is confirmed by an explicit human act, not inferred from the upload itself. Concretely, an upload enters a pending, non-retrievable state; an automated pass proposes answer_bearing and assessment_scope tags using the same criteria already established in Sections 4.1 and 9.1, including the begins-with-marker discipline that prevented the assessment_scope fix from silently degrading unrelated teaching content; a human, an operator initially, and potentially a specifically trained and trusted faculty role once real usage justifies building one, reviews and confirms or corrects the proposed tagging; and only upon that explicit confirmation does the content move to a live, retrievable state. This mirrors the access-mode default already established for course enrollment itself, closed until explicitly and deliberately opened, applied here to content rather than to student access.

Licensing confirmation is not waived or informalized for this path. A professor uploading a supplementary source, a reading, a licensed excerpt, must affirmatively confirm their institution's right to use that material in this context, the identical prerequisite Section 4.2 already requires of the operator path, before the content enters the tagging queue at all. A professor's good faith is not a substitute for this confirmation, the same way an operator's careful reading of a course was not, on its own, a substitute for the adversarial testing that eventually closed the real leak in reviewed content.

Identity verification for this path is the same requirement already stated for the escalation-configuration screen in Section 11.2.6, and for the same reason: whoever uploads content for a course must be independently confirmed as an authorized instructor or content owner for that specific course, not merely a logged-in user of the faculty dashboard generally. This path must not exist until Section 12.3a's faculty authentication gap resolves to something capable of making that specific, course-scoped guarantee.

What this path deliberately does not attempt to solve yet. It does not specify a workflow for a professor removing or superseding content already live, versioning and the deduplication discipline in Section 4.6 govern that separately and should be revisited once this path is closer to being built. It does not specify what happens when automated tagging and human review disagree beyond simple correction, a persistent disagreement pattern may itself be a signal worth surfacing to an operator rather than resolved silently case by case. And it does not resolve whether a faculty reviewer role, distinct from an operator, should ever be trusted to confirm tagging without an operator in the loop; that is a real future question and should be answered only once enough professor-contributed content exists to know whether the operator review step is a bottleneck worth removing or a safeguard worth keeping exactly as strict as it is today.

## 12.3 Access

At launch, this backend is accessible only to TCI's founder, or a narrowly named small set of trusted operators. It is not customer-facing and is not part of the faculty dashboard's access model. Any future expansion of access, additional TCI staff, a client's own limited administrative view of their own courses, is a deliberate future decision, not an assumed default.

## 12.3a Real authentication and login: a current gap, not a design decision

Both this operator backend and the faculty dashboard specified in Section 11.2 have been designed and wireframed as interfaces, but neither has a real, working login system behind it yet. This is worth naming as an explicit, current gap rather than an implicit assumption, since the two dashboards need genuinely different authentication postures, not a single shared login screen.

Operator authentication. A small, fixed set of trusted individuals, the founder and any named operators, per Section 12.3, need real accounts with real credentials, not a shared password or an informal access method. This is a narrow, low-user-count authentication need and does not require a public-facing signup flow, consistent with this backend's deliberately non-customer-facing scope.

Faculty and institutional authentication. A faculty member accessing their own course's dashboard per Section 11.2 needs an account scoped strictly to that course, with no visibility into any other course or client's data, the same isolation discipline already enforced at the database level for course content itself. As licensee institutions are eventually onboarded, this may need to integrate with an institution's own identity system rather than TCI issuing every credential directly, a decision to make deliberately once a real institutional client makes it concrete, not before.

Neither of these is the same authentication system used for students. Student identity arrives through the LTI launch, the popout token, or the widget's declared course identifier, per Section 6, none of which involve a student ever creating an account or logging into anything resembling this operator or faculty access. Conflating student-facing identity with staff-facing login would be a real design error, since the two serve entirely different trust and access purposes.

> **Note:** This gap belongs in the specification, not just a to-do list Neither dashboard is real to a user until someone can actually log into it. Wireframes and data models exist for both; a working, tested authentication layer for each does not. This should be treated as required infrastructure before either dashboard can be considered a real, usable product, not a finishing touch added after the rest of each dashboard is built.

## 12.4 Relationship to SaaS readiness

This backend is the natural seam where a future SaaS product would eventually live, an operator's console is structurally close to what a multi-tenant platform's internal admin tooling looks like. Building it now, scoped to TCI's own actual current need, one operator, one growing catalog, keeps that future path open without committing to build billing, self-serve signup, or client-facing account management before there is a paying client to justify them. The architectural discipline is: keep course and client identifiers cleanly separable now, and defer everything that only matters once a second party is actually paying for access.

# 13. The Tutoring Parameter Model: What the Research Actually Supports

A companion research synthesis and an expanded parameter specification were produced to answer a specific question: what does published tutoring research actually support building, as distinct from what sounds sophisticated. Both documents carry the same citation discipline already governing this project, every claim is graded by evidence strength, and every citation drawn from model knowledge rather than a verified research pass is marked as unverified and forbidden from entering any client-facing document until independently confirmed. This section records the findings that change or confirm this specification's existing decisions, not a restatement of the full parameter catalog, which remains a separate working document.

## 13.1 The central finding: construction beats explanation, and giving an answer has a real cost

The most robust finding across the literature is that tutoring works primarily by forcing a student to construct and articulate reasoning, not by the tutor delivering explanations. The clearest causal evidence for the risk this creates comes from a randomized controlled trial in which unguided access to an AI system reduced students' later unaided performance by seventeen percent relative to a guarded condition, despite improving in-session performance, the exact failure mode where a system looks successful while quietly teaching dependency. This finding is the direct research grounding for a specific, newly identified requirement.

> **Note:** A load-bearing requirement this specification did not previously state explicitly An answer given to a student must never be the end of the interaction. The causal harm identified in the research above is specifically about an answer delivered with no requirement that the student do anything with it afterward. An answer followed by a mandatory request that the student explain it back is a functionally different intervention from an answer given alone, and the research treats this as the single highest-leverage guardrail available. Any bottom-out disclosure, per Section 5's grading wall and the assistant's answer-of-last-resort behavior, must be followed by a required construction step, asking the student to restate or apply what was just revealed, before the interaction is considered closed.

## 13.2 The catalog risk: most of TCI's real courses are not the kind of course this research is about

The evidence base this literature rests on is drawn overwhelmingly from well-structured procedural domains: algebra, physics mechanics, programming, domains with a canonical solution sequence where a step is verifiably right or wrong. TCI's actual catalog, marketing research, business strategy, healthcare management, education coursework, is substantially ill-structured: there is no canonical step three in a marketing segmentation analysis, and quality is a matter of rubric-dimensional judgment, not sequential correctness. Applying the procedural research's architecture unmodified to this catalog risks a tutor that invents fake canonical steps and grades a student against them, which is worse than no tutor at all.

The practical consequence is that the assistant needs two distinct behavioral profiles, selected per course, not one architecture ported unmodified everywhere. A procedural profile diagnoses missing or buggy steps against a verifiable solution. An ill-structured profile diagnoses missing considerations, weak evidence, or unstated assumptions against a rubric, never reveals a conclusion, only a consideration the student has not yet addressed, and rests on a meaningfully weaker evidence base that should be disclosed honestly in any client-facing claim about the assistant's effectiveness, not presented with the same confidence as the procedural profile.

## 13.3 Why Phase 3 must precede deeper investment in the pedagogical layer, restated on new grounds

This specification already prioritizes real, verified student identity over further extension of the tutoring and memory layer, previously on architectural grounds, since the memory and adaptive-retrieval work already built depends on a placeholder identity that risks embedding wrong assumptions. The research review surfaces a second, independent, and arguably stronger reason for the same conclusion. Every metric this system can currently collect, in-session correctness, engagement, whether a student says they are satisfied, will look excellent for a tutor that is quietly teaching dependency, exactly the failure mode the causal research above identifies. The only instrument that reliably detects this is a comparison between a student's performance with the tutor available and their performance without it, run across a real population of distinct, trackable students. That comparison is impossible without real identity to assign and track students by, which means the pedagogical layer cannot currently be evaluated for harm at all, only assumed safe. Real identity is therefore not just an architectural prerequisite for personalization, it is the precondition for knowing whether personalization is helping or quietly harming.

## 13.4 Sequencing discipline: a research-grounded expansion is not a build mandate

The parameter specification this section summarizes catalogs a substantial number of tuning parameters across the assistant's behavior, deliberately organized into a small, high-priority set carrying most of the achievable effect, and a much larger set that should remain frozen at documented default values until real usage data justifies tuning them. This mirrors a standing principle already governing this project in other contexts: a control that cannot yet be measured should not be actively tuned, since doing so produces a system that looks more sophisticated while being no more verifiably correct, and harder to debug when something goes wrong. The correct posture toward the full parameter catalog is therefore selective and sequenced, not comprehensive, consistent with every other expansion this specification has undergone.

> **Note:** What this section deliberately does not do This section records the specific findings that change or confirm existing decisions in this specification. It does not reproduce the full parameter catalog, the complete research citation list, or the detailed evidence grading for every individual parameter, which remain in their own working document specifically so that document can be revised, expanded, or corrected on its own schedule without forcing a new version of this specification for every tuning change. Citations drawn from that document that have not yet been independently verified must not be treated as confirmed simply because they now appear referenced here.

## 13.5 A standing design principle, named after recurring three times under three different names

A single failure shape surfaced three separate times during the build session that produced v3.6, each time in a different component, before it was named as a general principle rather than fixed three times as three unrelated bugs. The distress-detection classifier's original error handling discarded the cause of an API failure and returned the same value it returns for no distress detected, meaning a total outage and a genuinely calm student were indistinguishable to everything downstream. Escalation configuration, per the correction to Section 12.2a above, allowed a course to carry a real recipient while still reading as disabled, or to read as enabled with no recipient at all, either state looking like working configuration while providing no actual protection. And a background memory-write path logged its own failure only to a console that a deployed serverless environment does not durably retain, meaning a lost interaction record and a successfully recorded one were indistinguishable after the fact.

The common shape: a component whose failure mode produces output identical or near-identical to its success mode, discoverable only by accident or by someone specifically auditing for it, exactly as all three were. The standing rule this specification now states directly, to be applied to every future component with a failure mode of this shape, not rediscovered piecemeal: any component whose silent failure would be indistinguishable from correct operation must fail in a way that is loud, and must record that failure somewhere durable and queryable, a table, not a log line depended on for anything safety- or compliance-relevant, since a log reaching only a console or a stream nobody is committed to reading is not meaningfully different from not logging at all.

This version adds verified implementation detail to Sections 3 and 4, a new deployment adapter in Section 6, and a new operator-facing system in Section 12, alongside the compliance spine from earlier versions, which remains unchanged. The three implementation findings recorded in Section 3 are worth re-reading before any further extension of the memory or retrieval system, since each represents a real failure mode a less careful build would have shipped without ever noticing.
